# Hebrew and Yiddish in genealogical sources

## Where they occur
- **Tombstones**: "פ״נ" (here lies, buried), the name, "בן/בת" (son of / daughter of) + the father's name, a date by the Jewish calendar, sometimes a surname and a town.
- **Metrical books of the Russian Empire**: the right-hand (Hebrew) part is often **fuller** than the Russian: the whole double name, the name of the groom's/bride's father.
- **Witness signatures** under records — in Hebrew/Yiddish; they give the name and surname of a literate person.
- **Newspapers in Yiddish and Hebrew** (19th–20th centuries): notices, correspondences, lists of donations.

## Name correspondences (common)
| Hebrew | Yiddish / everyday | In Russian documents | Later / in emigration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dov-Ber | Ber, Berl, Berko | Берко (Berko), Бер (Ber) | Борис (Boris), Bernard |
| Yisrael | Srol, Srul | Сруль (Srul), Израиль (Izrail) | Israel, Isidor |
| Eliezer | Leizer | Лейзер (Leizer), Лазарь (Lazar) | Leizer, Louis |
| Moshe | Moishe, Moshko | Мошко (Moshko), Моисей (Moisei) | Morris, Moses |
| Yitzhak | Itsik, Itsko | Ицко (Itsko), Исаак (Isaak) | Isaac |
| Shmuel | Shmil, Shmul | Шмуль (Shmul), Самуил (Samuil) | Samuel |
| Yehuda-Leib | Leib, Leiba | Лейба (Leiba), Лев (Lev) | Leo, Louis |
A double name (Berko-Duvid, Khaim-Iosi) is **one** name; the scribe might write down half of it. The patronymic in documents is "Мошков" (Moshkov), "Мошкович" (Moshkovich); in a newspaper "מאשקאוו".

**The Soviet period (1920s–1950s).** Names were often changed by sound or by the first letter: Khaim → Efim, Moishe → Mikhail, Berko → Boris, Khana → Anna. ⚠️ Often **only the children's patronymic** changed: in the father's documents it is still "Moishe", while the daughter is already "Mikhailovna". A child's patronymic cannot be converted straight back into the father's name — search for the father under both names. For more, and on changes of surname — the skill `soviet-era-records`.

## Dates
The Jewish calendar (month + day + year from the creation of the world, in letters): convert using tables/a converter; in metrical records beside it stands the "Christian" date **in the Old Style** (a difference of 12 days in the 19th century and 13 days from 1 March 1900).
- 🔑 **Check by the column which date stands in a boy's record.** In many books both dates are recorded ("born the 2nd, circumcised the 10th of April") — then take the date of birth. But if there is a single date, it is often the date of circumcision: boys were registered after the rite, and a separate birth date was entered in an atypical case (illness, postponement). Then the birth is roughly 7–8 days before the recorded date, and a discrepancy of a week or two from the family's date is not a contradiction. A repeat certificate from the rabbi carries over the same date (N. Lipes). The third date is the date of the registration itself: in a late registration it is months from the birth.
- ⚠️ **A Jewish day-number with a foreign month.** Biographers turned "25 Sivan" into "25 July": the day number from the Jewish date passes into the text with a Gregorian month. If a date is "strange", check whether its day number coincides with the day number of a Jewish month, and recalculate.

## How to read
1. First find the **structure** (on a tombstone — the formulae; in a metrical record — the column headings).
2. Read letter by letter, comparing with the same hand/typeface nearby (ד/ר, ה/ח, ו/ז/י, ס/ם — frequent confusions).
3. Check the name against name dictionaries (Beider, Feldblyum) and the surname against surname dictionaries.
4. Mark anything uncertain "(?)" and explain what prevents reading it.
5. Translate close to the text; keep the original spelling of names beside it.

## Customs that help in the search
- Ashkenazim named children after **deceased** relatives: the child's name suggests the deceased grandfather/grandmother and the approximate date of death.
- On a tombstone there is the **father's** name, which is a step up one generation.
- "Kohen"/"Levi" on a tombstone (hands, ewer) is a sign of lineage.
- Among Ashkenazim naming after **living** persons was not customary (a custom, not a law; exceptions occur), so full brothers have different names, and full namesakes "uncle — nephew" of close age are common (both named after one deceased person).
- **Yiddish signatures** under a record give a patronymic or a second surname that is not in the Russian text; a death record may name children whose births were not registered (the experience of JRI-Poland).

## If there was a rabbi or a synagogue official in the family
- **Prefaces, afterwords and approbations (*haskamot*) of rabbinic books** often set out the author's genealogy and mention other rabbinic families (HebrewBooks, the National Library of Israel). A family also calls a "rabbi" a merely learned man without a congregation.
- Reference works by place: "Ohole Shem" (1912) — rabbis by town; Deych's index "synagogues and their officials in the Pale of Settlement, 1853–1854" on JewishGen (jewishgen.org/databases/misc/deych.htm).
- Check traditional genealogies against tombstones and chronology, not against retelling.
