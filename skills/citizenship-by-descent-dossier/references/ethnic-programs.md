# Citizenship-based and descent/ethnicity-based routes

**Currency: sources checked 2026-11-18.** Source keys [S…] — at the end (same numbering as the other reference files). ⚠️ = secondary or not confirmed against the primary text.

> **Not legal advice.** Programme rules change, often yearly. Before filing, the user checks the conditions on the official page of the authority and records the date of checking.

## Two different questions

| | **Citizenship-based** route | **Descent / ethnicity-based** route |
|---|---|---|
| What is proved about the ancestor | that he **held the citizenship** of that state at a key date and did not lose it before the next link was born | that he **belonged to the nation** (nationality entry, language, religion, community) or simply **originated** from the territory |
| Typical evidence | passport, Heimatschein, registration in the population books, military papers, consular records, the 1935 census (Latvia) | the "nationality" column in Soviet documents, metrical records, church membership, community certificates, **the applicant's own language and declaration** |
| What breaks the chain | loss of citizenship (naturalisation abroad, marriage for women, deprivation) — see `citizenship-changes.md` | usually nothing in the past; the applicant's own **language test** or **declaration** fails, or the "nationality" in documents is different |
| Examples | Poland confirmation; Lithuania Art. 9; Latvia s. 2(1)1; Romania Art. 11; Germany Art. 116(2) GG | Germany *Spätaussiedler*; Hungary § 4(3) (partly); Lithuania Art. 10; Latvia s. 2(1)3; Karta Polaka; Israel |

The same family can fit both: check the citizenship route first (it usually needs no language), then the descent route as a fallback.

## Traps

1. **"My great-grandfather was German"** is a claim about ethnicity. It opens the *Spätaussiedler* route only for people **still living** in the former USSR and able to speak German; it does not give German citizenship to a descendant living in Israel or Europe. For citizenship through a German **citizen** ancestor, check Art. 116(2), § 15, § 5 StAG.
2. **A Jewish family from Galicia or Poland that lived in Germany before 1933** often had no German citizenship. Art. 116(2) does not fit; § 15 StAG (loss of habitual residence) may [S14].
3. **Karta Polaka is not citizenship** and it excludes families who repatriated to the USSR in 1944–1957 [S12]. Citizenship comes only after permanent residence in Poland [S11, Art. 30.1(7)].
4. **Lithuania Art. 10 vs Art. 9:** descent alone usually means renouncing the other citizenship [S21, Art. 10.2]; the citizenship route (Art. 9) allows dual citizenship for exiles, those who fled before 11.03.1990 and their descendants [S21, Art. 7, 9.2]. Check which one fits before advising.
5. **"Nationality" in Soviet documents** helps Israel and Karta Polaka; it does not prove **citizenship** for Poland, Lithuania or Latvia.
6. **Language routes are tests of the applicant, not of the ancestor:** Hungary, Latvia s. 2(1)3, Romania B1, Karta Polaka, Spätaussiedler. Ask about the user's language level early.
7. **Repealed or time-limited routes:** § 5 StAG ends 19.08.2031 [S14, § 5(3)]; Spain and Portugal Sephardic routes are closed (see `programs.md`).
8. **Laws change.** The Lithuanian text here is the 2010 version (amended in 2016 and 2022 ⚠️); the Polish and German texts are current consolidated versions on the date of checking.
