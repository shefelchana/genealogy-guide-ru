# Dating and attributing old photographs

## Principle

A single sign gives a wide spread of years; the **intersection of several independent signs** narrows it. This is Maureen Taylor's four-layer method ("Photo Detective", *Family Photo Detective*): technique, photographer, clothing, background. Each layer gives a "not earlier than — not later than" frame; the result is the intersection of the frames. If the layers disagree, that is a signal: a reprint, a photograph pasted on later, a misreading.

Attribution (who is in the photograph) is a **hypothesis** until a contemporary caption, a match with a captioned photograph and the family chronology all confirm it together. Record it in the words "probably …, because …".

## Step 0. What to ask the user for

- a scan or photo of **both sides**, even if the back is blank (embossing, glue marks, a stamp);
- the size of the card in millimetres, the colour and thickness of the cardboard, the shape of the corners (rounded / square);
- where the photograph comes from: who holds the original, from whose album, next to which photographs it lay;
- what the family says about it — and **who** is saying it.

Embossing on a mount is easier to see in a photograph taken with **side lighting**. Do not restore the original yourself, do not paste it in, and write on the back only in **soft pencil**.

## Step 1. Technique and format

The type of photograph sets the lower limit (Taylor):
- daguerreotype — from 1839;
- ambrotype — from 1854;
- ferrotype (tintype) — from 1856;
- carte de visite — after 1859.

Mount format (from a summary of search results; the accuracy is approximate):
- **visit portrait** (*vizit-portret*, carte de visite) — about 65×105 mm;
- **cabinet portrait** (*kabinet-portret*) — about 110×165 mm, in general use from the 1870s.

Cards with a postcard layout on the back are already 20th century; other layers date them more precisely.

## Step 2. Mount and reverse

Look at the front and the back:
- **embossing and printing**: the studio's name, city, address, "branch in …";
- **exhibition medals** with years — the card was printed **not earlier than** the year of the last medal;
- the **printer** who produced the cardboard, and the grade of the cardboard;
- **corners**: rounded or square;
- **spelling**: pre-reform (ѣ, і, ъ at the end of words) points to cardboard printed before the reform of 1917–1918; a studio could use up old stock, so this is a soft upper limit;
- Soviet-type names (artel, cooperative, state institution) — the 1920s and later.

For the mount as a whole, according to a summary of search results, the accuracy is about ±5 years.

## Step 3. The studio: years of operation

If the photographer's surname or the city can be read, find the studio's years of operation (details in `references/photo-directories.md`):
- **FotoTikon** — a directory of photographers of the Russian Empire 1839–1917, about 9,500–10,000 names, alphabetical, with cities and years;
- the **"Bolshoi Russkii Albom"** (Great Russian Album) (rusalbom.ru) — a catalogue of studios **by city**, with addresses and sometimes years ("in 1870", "ca. 1880"); convenient when the city is known but the surname is hard to read;
- **A. P. Popov, "Rossiiskie fotografy (1839–1930)"** (Russian Photographers) — about 16,000 names, an electronic version on the website of the Museum of Russian Photography.

If the years coincide in two directories, you have a frame. A studio could work for decades and change its address once: the **address** often dates more precisely than the name. There is no separate database of Jewish studios in shtetls (*mestechki*) — look for the studio of the shtetl or of the nearest district town (*uezdnyi gorod*) in the same directories.
🔑 The studio's city is also a **hint about the place** if the family's place of residence is unknown: a participant in the "Rod'N'ya" project (Am ha-Zikaron) learned from the studio address on a mount which city his great-grandmother came from. This is a hypothesis about the place, to be checked against the documents of that city.

If a directory page requires a login or a CAPTCHA, **the search on the site is done by a human**; you suggest what to enter and which spelling variants to check (pre-reform, Latin on cards marked "Photographie …"). Write down the years found in a directory with the name of the directory and a link.

## Step 4. Clothing, hairstyles, background

- **Fashion** changes quickly, especially women's: sleeves, silhouette, collars, hairstyles. Compare with **dated** photographs of the same region and the same milieu — best of all with captioned photographs from the same album.
- Allow for the milieu: in the provinces, in a shtetl, with traditional Jewish clothing, older people often lag behind fashion — dating from the elderly in a photograph is worse than from the young.
- **Military uniform and insignia** date well if they can be identified with confidence (for example, shoulder boards were introduced in the Red Army in 1943, before that there were collar tabs). Check the identification of a uniform against uniform reference books, not by eye.
- **Background, furniture, props**: studios changed backdrops and chairs. An identical backdrop in two photographs means one studio and, most likely, close years.

Do not name a specific year from clothing without a dated example to lean on. Give a range.

## Step 5. Inscriptions

- Copy the inscription **word for word**, with its spelling, abbreviations, and any unread place in square brackets: «На память дорогой [Ри?]вѣ отъ…» ["As a keepsake to dear [Ri?]va from…"]. Give your interpretation separately.
- Distinguish a **contemporary inscription** (a dedication, ink, the same hand as on the person's other letters) from a **late caption written from memory** (ballpoint pen, «это бабушка Маня» ["this is grandmother Manya"]). The first is evidence, the second is a guide; neither is proof.
- A date in an inscription before February 1918 is by the Julian calendar. An inscription in Yiddish or Hebrew may contain a date by the Jewish calendar — for reading, see the skill `reading-hebrew-yiddish-sources`.
- Even a wrong caption is useful: it shows whom the family believed to be pictured.

## Step 6. Who is in the photograph

1. **Chronology.** For each person estimate an age range and compare it with the dating and the birth years of the candidates. A photograph of around 1885 with an elderly couple cannot show people born in the 1870s. Check the number of children and their ages in a family photograph against the metrical records.
2. **Comparison with captioned photographs** of the same family: the same faces at another age, the same studio, the same backdrop. Compare stable features (the shape of the ears, the hairline, the set of the eyes), not facial expression.
3. **Ask relatives** — one photograph at a time: "who is this? where? on what occasion?". Write down who said what.
4. **Pitfalls:** sisters and brothers look alike, and a father and son at the same age get confused across a generation; photographs in an album may have been given by other families; one card may be a copy of someone else's photograph. Face-recognition programs give a hint, not a conclusion.

Do not name a person in a photograph in a definite way unless it is confirmed by at least two independent signs.

## Step 7. Record the result

For each photograph fill in `assets/photo-record.md`:
- where the photograph comes from and who has the original;
- size, type, format, studio, city, inscriptions — word for word, from both sides;
- dating — a range and **on which signs** each limit rests;
- who is pictured, **who thinks so** and on what basis;
- file names of the scans of both sides.

Keep scans as a master copy in TIFF and a working copy in JPEG; the rights to a photograph belong to the photographer — publishing other people's photographs requires permission (see the skill `writing-family-history`).

## Answer format

1. What is visible on the photograph and on the back (inscriptions word for word).
2. A table of layers: sign → frame of years → where the information comes from.
3. The overall range and how confident it is.
4. Who may be in the photograph — hypotheses with arguments for and against.
5. What to do to narrow it: which directory to check, which captioned photograph to find, whom to ask.

Do not invent the years of a studio's operation: if it has not been checked against a directory, say so. Answer in the user's language.
