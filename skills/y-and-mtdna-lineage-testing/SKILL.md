# Y-DNA and mtDNA: testing the direct lines

Y-DNA passes from father to son almost unchanged; mtDNA from mother to all her children. Each follows **one line only** out of hundreds of ancestors. That makes them strong for one narrow question ("are these two surname families one male line?") and useless for most others.

**Facts as of November 2026.** Products and prices change; give dates and suggest checking the company page. The user orders, pays and manages kits.

## Step 1. Is a direct-line test the right tool?

| Question | Test |
|---|---|
| Are two families with the same surname one male line? Is "our" surname line the one in this village? | **Y-DNA** |
| Did the surname change (adoption, wife's surname taken, a new surname given by the 1804–1850 laws)? | **Y-DNA** finds relatives with **other** surnames |
| The direct maternal line | **mtDNA** (expect matches far back in time) |
| Find relatives on all sides, confirm a grandparent, place a match | **autosomal** (other skills) |

Who tests: **a living man of the direct male line** for Y (father, brother, uncle, male cousin with the surname); anyone for their own mother's line (mtDNA). A woman cannot test her father's Y line herself — she needs a male relative.

To check a **documented** paternal line, test two men whose documented descent from the common ancestor is **as far apart as possible**. If they match, the paper line is supported; if not, there is a break (non-paternity, adoption, an error in documents) somewhere — but DNA does not say where.

## Step 2. Choose the level (2026)

| Option | What it gives | When |
|---|---|---|
| Haplogroup from an autosomal test (FamilyTreeDNA Family Finder NGS since March 2026; MyHeritage WGS read by third parties) | a confirmed mid-level Y haplogroup, **no Y matches** | a cheap first look; to choose whether to go deeper |
| **Y-37 / Y-111** (FamilyTreeDNA, STR) | matches with genetic distance; surname projects | surname questions within the last ~10 generations; the largest Y database |
| **Big Y-700** (FamilyTreeDNA, SNP + STR) | terminal subclade, SNP matches, branch on the Y tree | to split lines that share STR results; to date branches |
| **Whole genome 30x** (~$300) + YFull / TheYTree / dnachron | full Y and mtDNA, autosomal file convertible for other sites | the "do once" option forum regulars recommend; but these third-party trees do not have FamilyTreeDNA's STR match database |
| **mtFull** (FamilyTreeDNA) | full mitochondrial sequence, Mitotree haplogroup, mt matches | maternal-line questions; HVR1/HVR2 should be upgraded to full |

There is an open debate (late 2026): "Y-37 is a waste, go straight to WGS 30x" versus "FamilyTreeDNA has the biggest Y database and only STR/Big Y gives Y matches and projects". Present both; the deciding factor is whether the user needs **matches with other testers** (FamilyTreeDNA) or **only the branch** (WGS + third-party tree).

## Step 3. Projects and the profile

- Join the **surname project** and the **haplogroup project**; for Eastern Europe also regional projects.
- Fill in **"earliest known ancestor" with place of birth** (village, district, province, country). Without it project tables show "Unknown" and nobody can connect the line.
- Attach or link a tree.

## Step 4. Read Y-STR results correctly

- **Genetic distance (GD)** = number of mutation steps between two men's markers. FamilyTreeDNA shows matches only up to: GD 2 at Y-25, 4 at Y-37, 7 at Y-67, 10 at Y-111.
- **GD is not generations** and not "4th cousins" for GD 4. Markers mutate at different speeds; two men can be GD 0 with a common ancestor 10 generations back, or GD 3 with one 4 generations back.
- Old FamilyTreeDNA guidance for Y-111 (cited 2020, rough): GD 0 — most often 3rd cousins or closer; GD 1 — 5th cousins or closer; GD 2 — about 7th; GD 3 — about 9th; GD 4 — about 10th.
- **TiP** (FamilyTreeDNA calculator) shows where to start looking for the common ancestor, not a proof.
- **Convergence:** unrelated lines can look the same on few markers by chance — another reason for Big Y when it matters.
- A list of **1,800 matches at Y-37** is a reason to filter (by GD, by surname, by place), not evidence.
- A close Y match **may not show up** in an autosomal test, and the reverse.
- **Same haplogroup ≠ related.** A mid-level haplogroup can be thousands of years old. Kinship in genealogical time needs the **terminal subclade** (Big Y / WGS level), a branch date, **and** documents.
- **A Big Y match can be older than surnames** (before 1500–1800 in Eastern Europe). Say so when the matching man has a different surname from another country.

## Step 5. Read mtDNA results correctly

- A full-sequence match may mean a common ancestor 1–50 generations back. Most mt matches **cannot be traced** in documents.
- Haplogroups from autosomal chips and imputed files (some Russian services) are unreliable for comparison; only the full sequence (mtFull, WGS) is.
- Mitotree (launched February 2025, large update November 2025) renamed many haplogroups; compare results under the same tree version.
- Children of one mother **must** have the same mtDNA; different sub-branches between a mother and child from chip tests are chip artefacts (see the guide's DNA section, `guide/2-справочник/15-днк.md` § 6а; English: `en/guide/2-reference/15-dna.md` § 6a).

## Step 6. Tie it to documents and record the level of evidence

1. Put the two (or more) tested lines next to their **documents**: revision lists, family lists, metrical books, surname clusters of the village (see `tracing-revision-records`, `reconstructing-family-clusters`).
2. Write the conclusion with a level:
   - 🟢 documents + Y match at a level consistent with the documented distance;
   - 🟡 Y match fits, but documents have a gap;
   - 💭 haplogroup only or STR at a high GD;
   - ⚪ inconclusive; 🔴 contradicts (no match where documents say one male line).
3. In the log: who tested (initials), which test and level, GD or SNP status, project, date, and what question it answered.

## Common mistakes to warn about
- Expecting a Y test to show "where my great-grandmother came from".
- Expecting an X match with a paternal aunt for a man (a man's X comes only from his mother).
- Taking a shared haplogroup as proof that two families are one.
- Reading GD as generations.
- Ordering Y for a woman's own kit.
- Leaving "earliest known ancestor" empty.
