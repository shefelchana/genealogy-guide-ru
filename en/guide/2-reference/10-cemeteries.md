# Cemeteries and gravestones

What this section covers: how to use Jewish cemeteries as a source — find out where a person might have been buried and whether the cemetery survives, find a burial in databases, read a gravestone (*matzevah*) and convert a Jewish date. Site cards with tricks are in the [catalog](../../catalog/cemeteries.md).

The markers in square brackets ([checked], [from search snippet], [unverified] and others) are explained in the [Glossary](../0-start/06-glossary.md).

---

## 1. What a gravestone gives you

A matzevah often tells you what is not in the metrical books:
- the **father's name** ("ben …" — son of so-and-so, "bat …" — daughter of so-and-so) — a step up a generation;
- the exact **date of death** by the Jewish calendar;
- membership of the **Kohanim or Levites**;
- sometimes the surname and the town.

But the coverage of all cemetery databases is incomplete, and many cemeteries have been destroyed. So "not found in the database" is not a conclusion.

## 2. Where a person might have been buried

Search by the **place of death**, not of birth. For each place check:

1. **Whether there was a Jewish cemetery there.** The JewishGen Cemetery Discovery Project (cdp.jewishgen.org, formerly an IAJGS project) is a catalogue of burial places: region → country → locality; for each locality, the history of the community, the condition, sometimes photos [checked].
2. **Whether it survives.** ESJF surveys (esjf-cemeteries.org): 4,140 surveyed cemeteries [checked]; the database covers Poland, Ukraine, Moldova, Lithuania, Slovakia, Hungary, Bulgaria and others; Belarus and Latvia were not found in it [checked]. For each cemetery there is a status (demolished and built over / demolished, not built over / fenced), the number of stones, the **dates of the oldest and the newest stone**, coordinates. If the oldest stone is from 1885 and the person died in 1850, his stone is most likely not there.
3. **Whether there were transfers.** For example, the Lukianivka Jewish cemetery in Kyiv was closed in 1962; some of the burials were moved to Berkovtsy [from search snippet].

## 3. Burial databases by region

| Where he died | Where to start |
|---|---|
| Ukraine, Belarus, Russia, Moldova, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan | Mitzvat Emet (מצוות אמת; photos of stones), Toldot (search by part of a surname), JOWBR |
| Moscow | the register of the *chevra kadisha* (hevkad.com/burial: Vostryakovo, Malakhovka, Saltykovka cemeteries), Toldot |
| St Petersburg | the database of the Preobrazhenskoe Jewish cemetery [from search snippet] |
| Poland | Foundation for Documentation of Jewish Cemeteries (cemetery.jewish.org.pl), JOWBR |
| Bessarabia | the cemetery project of Bessarabia SIG; Chișinău — 18,751 records and 14,150 photos in JOWBR [from search snippet] |
| Israel | search each local *chevra kadisha* separately (Tel Aviv, Rehovot, Haifa, Jerusalem…) — **in Hebrew**; BillionGraves: according to a MyHeritage announcement (2019), almost all cemeteries in Israel have been photographed, later burials may be missing [from search snippet] |
| USA | Find a Grave, BillionGraves, JOWBR; the JGSNY database of plots of societies of people from the same town (*landsmanshaftn*; more than 10,600 records from almost 100 cemeteries of the New York area) [checked] |
| Worldwide | JOWBR (JewishGen): more than 7 million records, about 9,500 cemeteries in 143 countries [checked]; a free login is needed |

How to search:
- all spellings of the surname and the name, including Soviet forms (Khaim → Yefim) and Hebrew ones;
- in Mitzvat Emet: type the beginning of the surname, **choose the suggestion**, press "Search". After about five searches in a row the form silently stops submitting (protection against robots). Don't get around it — continue later, one search at a time;
- captchas, logins and registration are done by a person.

## 4. How to read a matzevah

The order of reading (a detailed table of formulas is in the skill [`researching-jewish-cemeteries`](../../../skills/researching-jewish-cemeteries/SKILL.md), in Russian):

1. At the top **פ״נ** (here lies) or **פ״ט** (here is hidden).
2. Epithets: **ר׳** — "Reb", a polite form of address, **not** a rabbi; **מו״ה** — "our teacher, Mr.", a mark of learning; **הבתולה** — an unmarried girl; **האשה החשובה** — "the respected woman", married; **הילד/הילדה** — a child.
3. The name, then **בן / בת** and the father's name (often **ב״ר** — "son of Reb …"); **הכהן / הלוי** — Kohen or Levite.
4. Surname and town — not always.
5. The date: day, month, year in letters (gematria), often with **לפ״ק** — "by the small count", without the thousands.
6. At the end **ת.נ.צ.ב.ה.** — "may his soul be bound up in the bond of life".

**Symbols.** Hands in the gesture of blessing — a Kohen; a pitcher — a Levite; candlesticks — a woman; books — a scholar; a broken tree or candle — an early death. Animals often **encode the name**: a deer — Tsvi, Hirsh, Naftali; a lion — Arye, Leib, Yehuda; a bear — Dov, Ber; a bird — Tsipora, Feiga.

**Soviet monuments** (1920s–1980s): a Russian text with the full name and dates, an enamel photo, sometimes one line in Hebrew with the Jewish name and the father's name. Compare the Russian patronymic with the Hebrew name of the father: "Borisovich" (Борисович) and "בן דוב בער" are one person. It happens that the name in the documents and the name on the stone cannot be reconciled either through Russification or through a double name. Then write both forms side by side, without fitting one to the other.

## 5. How to convert the date

Year in letters + 5000 (if the thousands are omitted) − 3760 = Gregorian year. For the months Tishrei, Cheshvan, Kislev and part of Tevet — **one year less**. In a leap year find out whether it is **Adar I or Adar II**. Example: 12 Kislev 5665 is 20 November 1904, and 12 Nisan of the same year is 17 April 1905.

Converters: Hebcal (hebcal.com/converter), Steve Morse's converter for gravestones (stevemorse.org/jcal/tombstone.html) [checked]. To compare with Russian documents, convert to the Julian calendar: −12 days in the 19th century, −13 from 1 March 1900 to February 1918. More in [Handwriting, transliteration, dates](05-handwriting-transliteration-dates.md).

## 6. Neighbours, plots, books

- **Neighbouring stones** are often kin: spouses side by side, children by their parents, family enclosures. Photograph and read the neighbours.
- **Plots of societies of people from one town** in the USA (*landsmanshaftn*) name the shtetl of origin. This is an argument, not proof: people from neighbouring places joined the society too.
- In Israel and in Soviet cemeteries the plots went by year of burial: the plot number narrows the year.
- **The record books of burial societies** (*chevra kadisha*) survive in single copies; the known ones are held in YIVO and the National Library of Israel [from search snippet].
- **A destroyed cemetery**: look for lists made before demolition (local historians, ESJF, JOWBR), photographs in memorial books and photographs of funerals in family albums ([Old photographs](17-photographs.md)).
- **A pedigree "from a great rabbi"** is checked with stones: the fathers' names on gravestones and the chronology of the generations must agree with the family story.

## 7. Photos on site

You can photograph a stone yourself, or a volunteer can. How to shoot: the whole stone and the inscription close up, in side light; both sides; the neighbouring stones; a general view with a landmark; coordinates. Do not clean the stone with chemicals and do not trace it with chalk.

## 8. What counts as a find and how to record "not found"

- **A find**: a photo of the stone (or a database record with a link), a verbatim transcription, a translation, the date in both calendars, a match of **at least two** features with a known person (name + father's name, name + date, name + place).
- **Not found**: list the databases, spellings, cemeteries and their status. For example: "not found in databases X, Y under spellings A, B; the cemetery is partly destroyed; no conclusion about the burial place is drawn".

> **How to ask AI**
>
> *For a plain chat, add: "If you can't open a source, say so; mark fonds, villages and dates you name from memory as [from memory, verify]. Answer in English, and give names, places and archive titles in the original script in parentheses." ([why](../1-ai-search/02-prompt-library.md)). The (browser agent) label is for an AI that opens websites itself; the other prompts suit any chat.*
>
> - "Read the inscription on this gravestone [photo]: a transcription line by line in the original, a translation, the father's name, Kohen or Levite, the date by the Jewish calendar and its conversion into the Gregorian (and the Julian if before 1918). What do the symbols on the stone mean?" *Check yourself:* the date — in the Hebcal or Morse converter; Adar I or II in a leap year; the letters of the name — against an enlarged photo.
> - "A person died in [place] in [year]. Where can I check whether there was a Jewish cemetery there, whether it survives, which years of stones are known, and in which databases to look for the burial? Mark whatever you can't check as [from memory, verify]." *Check yourself:* on the pages of the JewishGen Cemetery Discovery Project and ESJF.
>
> *Bad → good:* "Where is [name] buried?" (it will invent a cemetery; no place or year of death) → "[Name] died in [place] in [year]: in which databases should I look for the burial, and does the cemetery there survive?"
