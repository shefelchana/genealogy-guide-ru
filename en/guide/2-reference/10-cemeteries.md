# Cemeteries and gravestones

What this section covers: how to use Jewish cemeteries as a source — to find out where a person might have been buried and whether the cemetery survives, to find a burial in databases, to read a gravestone (*matzevah*) and to convert a Hebrew date. Cards for the sites, with tips, are in the [catalog](../../catalog/cemeteries.md).

Markers: **[checked]** — the page was read (October 2026); **[from search snippet]** — information taken from search results; **[unverified]** — exactly that.

---

## 1. What a gravestone gives

A matzevah often tells you what the metrical books do not:
- **the father's name** ("ben …" — son of so-and-so, "bat …" — daughter of so-and-so) — a step up one generation;
- the exact **date of death** by the Jewish calendar;
- membership of the **Kohanim or Levites**;
- sometimes a surname and a town.

But the coverage of all cemetery databases is incomplete, and many cemeteries have been destroyed. So "not found in the database" is not a conclusion.

## 2. Where a person might have been buried

Search by the **place of death**, not of birth. For each place check:

1. **Whether there was a Jewish cemetery there.** The JewishGen Cemetery Discovery Project (cdp.jewishgen.org, formerly an IAJGS project) is a catalogue of burial places: region → country → locality; a locality has the history of the community, the condition of the cemetery, sometimes photos [checked].
2. **Whether it survives.** Surveys by ESJF (European Jewish Cemeteries Initiative; esjf-cemeteries.org): 4,140 surveyed cemeteries [checked]; the database covers Poland, Ukraine, Moldova, Lithuania, Slovakia, Hungary, Bulgaria and others; Belarus and Latvia were not found in the database [checked]. A cemetery has a status (demolished and built over / demolished, not built over / fenced), a number of stones, **the dates of the oldest and the newest stone**, and coordinates. If the oldest stone is from 1885 and the person died in 1850, his stone is most likely not there.
3. **Whether there were transfers.** For example, the Lukianivka Jewish cemetery in Kyiv was closed in 1962; some burials were moved to the Berkovtsy cemetery [from search snippet].

## 3. Burial databases by region

| Where the person died | Where to start |
|---|---|
| Ukraine, Belarus, Russia, Moldova, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan | "Mitzvat Emet" (photos of stones), Toldot (search by part of a surname; Russian-language site), JOWBR |
| Moscow | the register of the chevra kadisha (burial society) (hevkad.com/burial: Vostryakovo, Malakhovka, Saltykovka; Russian-language site), Toldot |
| St Petersburg | database of the Preobrazhenskoe Jewish cemetery [from search snippet] |
| Poland | Foundation for Documentation of Jewish Cemeteries (cemetery.jewish.org.pl), JOWBR |
| Bessarabia | the cemetery project of the Bessarabia SIG; Chisinau — 18,751 records and 14,150 photos in JOWBR [from search snippet] |
| Israel | search each local chevra kadisha separately (Tel Aviv, Rehovot, Haifa, Jerusalem…) — **in Hebrew**; BillionGraves: per a MyHeritage announcement (2019), almost all Israeli cemeteries have been photographed; later burials may be missing [from search snippet] |
| USA | Find a Grave, BillionGraves, JOWBR; the JGSNY database of landsmanshaftn (hometown society) plots (more than 10,600 records from almost 100 cemeteries in the New York area) [checked] |
| Worldwide | JOWBR (JewishGen): more than 7 million records, about 9,500 cemeteries in 143 countries [checked]; free login required |

How to search:
- all spellings of the surname and given name, including Soviet forms (Khaim → Efim) and Hebrew ones;
- in "Mitzvat Emet": type the beginning of the surname, **select the suggestion**, press "Search". After about five searches in a row the form silently stops submitting (robot protection). Do not get around it — continue later, one search per visit;
- a person completes any captcha, login and registration.

## 4. How to read a matzevah

The order of reading (a detailed table of formulas is in the skill `researching-jewish-cemeteries`):

1. At the top **פ״נ** ("here lies") or **פ״ט** ("here is hidden").
2. Epithets: **ר׳** — "Reb", a polite form of address, **not** a rabbi; **מו״ה** — "our teacher, Mr.", a sign of learning; **הבתולה** — an unmarried girl; **האשה החשובה** — "the respected woman", married; **הילד/הילדה** — a child.
3. The name, then **בן / בת** ("son of" / "daughter of") and the father's name (often **ב״ר** — "son of Reb …"); **הכהן / הלוי** — a Kohen or a Levite.
4. A surname and a town — not always.
5. The date: day, month, year in letters (gematria), often with **לפ״ק** — "by the minor reckoning", without the thousands.
6. At the end **ת.נ.צ.ב.ה.** — "may his soul be bound up in the bond of life".

**Symbols.** Hands in the gesture of blessing — a Kohen; a pitcher — a Levite; candlesticks — a woman; books — a scholar; a broken tree or candle — an early death. Animals often **encode the name**: a deer — Tsvi, Hirsh, Naftali; a lion — Arye, Leyb, Yehuda; a bear — Dov, Ber; a bird — Tsipora, Feyga.

**Soviet monuments** (1920s–1980s): Russian text with the full name and dates, an enamel photo, sometimes one line in Hebrew with the Jewish name and the father's name. Compare the Russian patronymic with the Hebrew father's name: "Borisovich" and "בן דוב בער" ["son of Dov Ber"] — the same person. Sometimes the name in documents and the name on the stone cannot be reconciled either through Russification or through a double name. Record both forms side by side as they are, without forcing one to fit the other.

## 5. How to convert the date

The year in letters + 5000 (if the thousands are omitted) − 3760 = the Gregorian year. For the months Tishrei, Cheshvan, Kislev and part of Tevet — **one year less**. In a leap year, establish whether it is **Adar I or Adar II**. Example: 12 Kislev 5665 is 20 November 1904, while 12 Nisan of the same year is 17 April 1905.

Converters: Hebcal (hebcal.com/converter), Steve Morse's converter for gravestones (stevemorse.org/jcal/tombstone.html) [checked]. To compare with Russian documents, convert to the Julian calendar: −12 days in the 19th century, −13 from 1 March 1900 to February 1918. More in [Handwriting, transliteration, dates](05-handwriting-transliteration-dates.md).

## 6. Neighbours, plots, books

- **Neighbouring stones** are often relatives: spouses side by side, children by their parents, family enclosures. Photograph and read the neighbours.
- **Plots of hometown societies** in the USA (landsmanshaftn) name the shtetl of origin. This is an argument, not proof: people from neighbouring places also joined the society.
- In Israel and in Soviet cemeteries plots were allocated by year of burial: the plot number narrows down the year.
- **Minute books (pinkasim) of burial societies** (chevra kadisha) have survived only in a few cases; the known ones are held at YIVO and the National Library of Israel [from search snippet].
- **A destroyed cemetery**: look for lists made before demolition (local historians, ESJF, JOWBR), photos in memorial books, and photos of funerals in family albums ([Old photographs](17-photographs.md)).
- **A pedigree "from a great rabbi"** is tested by the stones: the fathers' names on gravestones and the chronology of the generations must agree with the family story.

## 7. Photographing on site

You can photograph a stone yourself, or a volunteer can. How to shoot: the whole stone and the inscription close up, in raking light; both sides; neighbouring stones; a general view with a landmark; coordinates. Do not clean the stone with chemicals and do not trace it with chalk.

## 8. What counts as a find, and how to record "not found"

- **A find**: a photo of the stone (or a database record with a link), a verbatim transcription, a translation, the date in both calendars, a match on **at least two** features with a known person (name + father's name, name + date, name + place).
- **Not found**: list the databases, spellings, cemeteries and their status. For example: "not found in databases X, Y under spellings A, B; the cemetery is partly destroyed; no conclusion is drawn about the place of burial".

> **How to ask AI**
>
> - "Read the inscription on this gravestone [photo]: transcription line by line in the original, translation, the father's name, Kohen or Levite, the date by the Jewish calendar and its conversion to the Gregorian (and Julian, if before 1918). What do the symbols on the stone mean?" *Check yourself:* the date — in the Hebcal or Morse converter; Adar I or II in a leap year; the letters of the name — on an enlarged photo.
> - "A person died in [place] in [year]. Was there a Jewish cemetery there, does it survive, which stone years are known, which databases should I search for the burial?" *Check yourself:* against the JewishGen Cemetery Discovery Project and ESJF pages.
> - "The stone says [name in Hebrew], and the documents say [Russian name and patronymic]. Could this be one person? Through which forms of the name (double name, everyday, Russian) are they connected?" *Check yourself:* a match not only of the name but also of the date or place. (The AI may need to read Russian-language sources.)
> - "Make a list of spellings of the surname [N] in Hebrew, Yiddish, Cyrillic and Latin for searching cemetery databases." *Check yourself:* the Hebrew forms — in databases with phonetic search.
