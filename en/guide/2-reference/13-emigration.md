# Emigration: from American documents to the shtetl

What this section covers: how to use the documents of an emigrant to the USA (and, briefly, to Canada, Argentina, Palestine, South Africa, Australia) to learn which shtetl he came from, and move on to documents in the homeland. The table of American sources, the ports and the departure lists are in [USA and emigration](../../catalog/usa-and-emigration.md).

Source markers: **[checked]** — read on the page itself (October 2026); **[from search snippet]** — only from search results, verify before an important decision. Prices and tariffs change — check on the sites.

## 1. Step-by-step algorithm

**1. Start from the end of life, in the USA.** Collect everything you have: death certificate, obituary, gravestone (photos of **both** sides: the Hebrew side has the father's name), naturalization certificate, letters. Write down all variants of the name (English, Yiddish, Hebrew), years of birth (they "float"), addresses, names of relatives and of neighbours from the same hometown.

**2. The death certificate and SS-5.**
- A death certificate usually has the place of birth and the parents' names. But the information comes from an informant, a relative, and may be inaccurate. For immigrants the parents are often recorded as "Unknown".
- SS-5 is the application for a Social Security number: it may contain the place of birth, the father's name and the mother's maiden name [checked: CJH]. It is requested from the SSA (Social Security Administration); the rules for releasing parents' names are strict [from search snippet].
- SSDI is the Social Security Death Index, mostly from 1962: name, dates of birth and death, last residence; free on FamilySearch [checked: CJH].

**3. Censuses 1900–1950.**
- The censuses of 1900, 1910, 1920, 1930 contain the **year of immigration and the citizenship status** [checked: CJH]. Status codes: **Al** — alien, **Pa** — filed "first papers", **Na** — naturalized [checked: NARA, 2026-10-07]. They suggest whether there is a naturalization and in which year to look for it.
- The censuses of 1910, 1920, 1930 record the **mother tongue**: "Yiddish" is a strong sign [checked: CJH].
- The 1940 census has no year of immigration (except the 5 percent sample) [checked: CJH]. The 1950 census has been open since 2022, free, searchable by name [checked].

**4. Naturalization is the main bridge to the shtetl.** The course of a case: Declaration of Intention ("first papers") → Petition ("second papers") → Certificate. The steps could take place in different courts [checked: NARA].
- **After 27.09.1906** the file gives a specific town of origin; the date, ship and port of arrival; the date of marriage; the names, dates and places of birth of the wife and children; the previous address abroad; the former name, if it was changed [checked: CJH].
- **Before 1906** — usually only the name, country, date of filing. Any court could naturalize: federal, state, county, city. There is no single index [checked].
- Until 22.09.1922 wives and children received citizenship through the head of the family, and no separate papers were created for them [checked: CJH].
- After 1906 the record of entry was checked against the manifest during naturalization, and the Certificate of Arrival in the file names **the ship and the date**, even if the immigrant himself had forgotten them (from the work of USCIS historian Marian Smith) [from search snippet].

**5. Find the arrival.** From the naturalization or census take the year and the ship. Search for the manifest with spelling variants and through the "sounds like" search on Steve Morse's forms (the Ellis Island Gold Form searches by town and by the name of a fellow traveller) [checked]. What a manifest gives:
- from 1893 — last residence, to whom he is travelling [from search snippet];
- from 1906 — place of birth [from search snippet];
- from 1907 — **the name and address of the nearest relative in the homeland** and to whom he is going in the USA [checked: MyHeritage Wiki].

**6. Check that it is the right person.** These must match:
- the name (allowing for transliteration);
- age ±2–3 years;
- the shtetl (check in the JewishGen Gazetteer or Communities Database);
- the relative in the homeland — with the parents or wife from other documents;
- the addressee in the USA — with the person the man lived with according to the census;
- fellow travellers — neighbouring lines of the manifest: brothers, sisters, people from the same hometown.

Check **on three points**, not on the name. A real example: the address from a naturalization card turned up in two censuses — this confirmed the identity.

**7. Narrow down the shtetl from indirect data, if there is no direct one:**
- **a landsmanshaft (hometown society) plot in a cemetery** — the society bought a plot, and the place of burial points to the town. JGSNY has a free database of such plots: more than 10,600 records from almost 100 cemeteries in the New York area [checked];
- **membership in a hometown society** — YIVO collections [checked: JGSNY];
- **the First World War draft card** (1917–1918): date and place of birth, citizenship [checked: NARA];
- **a passport application** (before 1925): place of birth, naturalization, arrival [checked: FamilySearch Wiki];
- **the 1942 draft of older men** ("old man's draft", registration 27.04.1942, those born from 27.04.1877 to 16.02.1897; place of birth and address) [checked: NARA, 2026-10-07].
- **New York marriage applications of 1908–1929** — about 450 thousand, scans put online by the Reclaim The Records project on the Internet Archive (archive.org/details/nycmarriageindex): the specific town of birth, the countries of birth of the parents, earlier marriages [checked];
- **HIAS arrival cards** (1909–1978) — the card shows the whole family who travelled together; held in the HIAS collection at YIVO (RG 245.4.41, Center for Jewish History), digitised [from search snippet: libguides.cjh.org];
- **recipients of aid from hometown societies** in the 1920s: the pogrom databases contain 1922 lists of who received money and from which hometown society. They can link an American branch to a specific family in the shtetl ([Pogroms 1918–1922](11-pogroms-1918-1922.md));
- **immigrant bank books** (in Philadelphia — at the Philadelphia Jewish Archives, Temple University): by the ticket order number a form is issued with the sender's address and the recipient's shtetl. In one study this route remained an unused lead — check it yourself.

**8. Departure from Europe.** According to the JewishGen FAQ, about 40% of Eastern European Jews left via Hamburg — the Hamburg lists should always be checked [checked]. The Hamburg lists before 1920 **do not state religion**: you cannot filter by "Jewish", search by name, age and place (J. Sielemann, former archivist of the Hamburg State Archive) [checked]. Knowing the port and year, look for the departure list: **Hamburg** (1850–1934, with the emigrant's hometown and relatives on the same ship), **Bremen** (mostly 1920–1939), **Rotterdam**, **Antwerp** (no lists, but there are police files on foreigners). Details — [USA and emigration](../../catalog/usa-and-emigration.md).

**9. Move on to documents in the homeland** using the shtetl and uezd found: metrical books, revisions, conscription lists ([Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](01-records-empire-ussr.md), [Archives by country](07-archives-by-country.md)). For verification take the parents and the relative from the 1907+ manifest.

**10. Record the level of proof.** The shtetl can be considered established if at least two documents name it **independently**: naturalization + manifest, or manifest + gravestone and hometown society.

## 2. Pitfalls

- **"The surname was changed at Ellis Island" is a myth.** Inspectors worked from manifests compiled by the steamship companies [checked: US National Park Service]. The manifest was filled in **before sailing**, in Europe, from the words of the emigrant or the ticket agent; so distortions in it come from a European clerk, and the place was written down by ear [from search snippet: M. Smith, S. Sack].
- **The American name** was usually chosen by the first sound of the Jewish one (Moshe → Max or Morris); look for the real name on the gravestone (JewishGen FAQ) [checked]. Immigrants changed names themselves, usually at naturalization. The new name is found in the naturalization file ("original name if changed").
- **A surname could change completely.** A real example: the emigrant Moische Shefel lived in the USA as Max Shechter. Search by place of birth, age, the relative he was going to, the mother's maiden name.
- **Transliteration.** The manifest was written in Europe: a German, Polish or Dutch rendering of a Russian surname (Sch-, W-, -ow, J- for Й). Use the Daitch–Mokotoff Soundex (at Morse's site).
- **Age "floats"** by 2–5 years between documents. Use a range.
- **The name of the shtetl is distorted** ("Pocolifoff" = Pokotilov, "Proskaw" = Proskurov). Check in the JewishGen Gazetteer and on Morse's forms ("sounds like", "contains").
- **Alternative ports.** At peak hours immigrants were sent to other ports, "even Canadian ones" [checked: CJH]. A negative result for one port proves nothing: check Baltimore, Philadelphia, Boston, Canada.
- **A look-alike surname in the index.** Real cases: the index read "Chaim Shefel", but on the frame it is Stiefel; a family tradition of arrival on the "Statendam" in 1905 was not confirmed by the frames; a naturalization card in the index is dated 1938, on the frame it is 1932. **Read the frame, not the index.**

## 3. Other countries (briefly)

- **Canada** — Library and Archives Canada: passenger lists 1865–1935; entry across the US border 1908–1935; the collection **"Immigrants from the Russian Empire, 1898–1922"** (documents of the Russian consulates in Montreal, Vancouver, Halifax: names and variants, photo, year of birth, religion); the series "Passport and Identity Papers" is digitised and free [checked].
- **Argentina** — CEMLA (arrivals at the port of Buenos Aires, more than 4.4 million people [from search snippet]); the Jewish colonies of Baron Hirsch — the **JCA Passenger Lists** database on JewishGen with the **place of origin in Europe** [checked].
- **Palestine / Israel** — the Israel State Archives: more than 60,000 naturalization files of 1937–1947 (place and date of birth, former nationality, photo, family, date of arrival); the full index is at MyHeritage, paid [checked: MyHeritage Wiki]. IGRA — more than 27,000 name changes from the Palestine Gazette 1917–1948 [checked]. The open dataset mandatenamechanges.org — 19,043 name changes of 1921–1947 with a link to the scan page for each record [checked]. If a branch went to Palestine, the name could have been officially Hebraised: compare the English and Hebrew versions of the notice.
- **South Africa** — SA Jewish Rootsbank (University of Cape Town): naturalizations, arrivals, about 15,000 families of 1850–1950, including from Lithuania, Latvia, Belarus; free [checked].
- **Australia** — National Archives of Australia: naturalization from 1904; the file has the date and ship of arrival, the town of birth, the family [from search snippet].

Links and details — [USA and emigration](../../catalog/usa-and-emigration.md).

> **How to ask AI**
>
> - "Here is a death certificate [text]. Which other documents should I look for (census, naturalization, ship's list, draft card), and what might each contain about the place of birth and the parents?" *Check yourself:* the name of the shtetl in American documents is distorted — check against the JewishGen Gazetteer. (The AI may need to read Russian-language sources.)
> - "Here is a place name from a manifest: '[as written]'. Which real shtetls could have been written down like this by ear? Give the uezd and guberniya for each variant." *Check yourself:* the manifest was compiled by a European clerk by ear; a second document is needed.
> - "Make a plan for searching for the emigrant [name, year of arrival, state]: in what order to look at the documents and where they are held." *Check yourself:* the dates of the document series and the ordering rules — on the NARA and USCIS sites.
