# Jewish genealogy: how things were organised

What this section covers: what you need to know about the life of the Jews of the Russian Empire to understand where documents are held. The Pale of Settlement, communities and rabbis, names, changing borders, the Jewish press and cemeteries.

---

## 1. The Pale of Settlement and the shtetls

Most Jews of the Russian Empire were allowed to live only in the western guberniyas — in the **Pale of Settlement** (today's Ukraine, Belarus, Lithuania, Moldova and eastern Poland). Life went on in **shtetls** (*mestechki*: small towns with a large Jewish community) and in cities.

For your search this means:
- almost all pre-revolutionary documents of a Jewish family lie in the archives of the **guberniyas of the Pale** (Podolia, Kyiv, Volhynia, Kherson, Bessarabia and others);
- if an ancestor lived outside the Pale before 1917 (for example, in Moscow or St Petersburg), look in the directories "Vsya Moskva" (All Moscow), "Ves Leningrad" (All Leningrad) and similar, medical lists, student lists — and still check the place of registration inside the Pale.

## 2. The community: *kahal*, "society", crown rabbi

- Until **1844** a Jewish community was governed by the **kahal**; afterwards the community was called a "**society**" (*obshchestvo*). This is why early documents are often grouped "by kahal" (the Polish censuses of the Jews of 1765–1791, for example, are arranged this way).
- From **1835** the metrical books were kept by **crown rabbis** (*kazyonnye ravviny*), appointed by the state, as distinct from the community's spiritual rabbis. So look for the fonds of the shtetls' "rabbinates". Example: the Central State Historical Archive of Ukraine in Kyiv (ЦДІАК, TsDIAK) has a list of the surviving rabbinates of Kyiv Guberniya with reference numbers and years (Berdychiv, Ruzhyn, Skvyra and about 70 others).

### If there was a "rabbi" in the family

- In family memory a "rabbi" can simply mean a learned man without a community. Rabbinical families did not include in their pedigrees sons and sons-in-law who did not become scholars (the Center for Jewish History guide "Rabbinical Genealogy") [checked].
- **Prefaces, afterwords and approbations (*haskamot*) of rabbinical books** often set out the author's ancestry and mention other rabbinical families. Secular genealogy usually does not read these books, and religious genealogy does not go to archives; the combination gives results (the Am HaZikaron institute, «Ам а-Зикарон»). The books are on HebrewBooks and in the National Library of Israel.
- Reference works: "Ohole Shem" (1912) — rabbis by town; Deych's index "synagogues and their officials in the Pale of Settlement, 1853–1854" on JewishGen (jewishgen.org/databases/misc/deych.htm) [checked].
- A family pedigree "from a great rabbi" is checked against gravestones and the chronology of the generations ([Cemeteries](10-cemeteries.md)). Pedigrees of famous dynasties are often based on family tradition, not on documents.

## 3. Estates and registration

Estate — *meshchane*, merchants, agricultural colonists, guild artisans — is a **status, not an origin**. Moving between estates was common. Documents are searched **by place of registration**, not place of residence. More in [Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](01-records-empire-ussr.md), the section "Estate and registration".

## 4. Names

- Surnames were fixed by the law of 1804, and finally by the 1830s.
- Double names (Berko-Duvid) are one name.
- Children were named after **dead** relatives.
- Documents contain everyday forms (Berko, Srul, Moshko) and Russian ones (Boris, Izrail, Mikhail).

All this is covered in detail in [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md).

## 5. Borders changed — and so did the archives

The same shtetl could belong to different states in different years. Then look for the documents in the archive of **that** state.

| Territory | What it was | What it means for your search |
|---|---|---|
| **Bessarabia** | from 1812 — Russian Empire | Russian metrical books, revisions (archives of Moldova — the National Archives of the Republic of Moldova, NARM; partly on FamilySearch) |
| **Southern Bessarabia** (Izmail, Kilia, Bolhrad, Cahul) | **1856–1878 — Romania** (the Principality of Moldavia) | someone born there in 1868 is recorded in **Romanian** civil registration, not a Russian metrical book. Akkerman remained on the Russian side in the same years |
| **Bessarabia** | 1918–1940 — Romania | Romanian civil registration records |
| **Galicia** | Austria (Austria-Hungary) until 1918 | Austrian metrical books; the databases of JRI-Poland, Gesher Galicia, AGAD |
| **Kingdom of Poland** | under Russia, but with its own system of vital records | JRI-Poland, Geneteka, Polish archives |
| **Transcarpathia** | Hungary until 1920; Czechoslovakia 1919–1938; Hungary again 1938/39–1944 | see also [Citizenship by descent](14-citizenship-by-descent.md) |

A real example. A man was born in Izmail in 1868. There is no Russian metrical book and there cannot be: the town was then Romanian. There are no Romanian records of Izmail for 1865–1878 on FamilySearch; the collection "Romania, Civil Registration 1865–1903" is very small. The guide to the Izmail archive (1966) confirms: there are no materials for 1856–1878. The absence of a record here is a property of the source, not a failure of the search.

## 6. The Jewish press

Newspapers in Hebrew and Yiddish of the 19th and early 20th centuries are one of the most underrated sources. The largest collection is the **Historical Jewish Press** of the National Library of Israel (NLI): Ha-Tsfira, Ha-Melitz, Ha-Maggid, Yidishes Folksblat, Forverts and others, with search in the recognised text.

What to look for there:
- **advertisements**: searches for missing husbands (a wife abandoned by her husband without a divorce — an *agunah* — could not remarry and looked for him through a newspaper), searches for relatives, weddings, mourning notices;
- **correspondence from the shtetls**: chronicle, scandals, weddings; often signed with a pseudonym;
- **donation lists**: full names, sometimes with patronymic and place of residence. A real example: a correspondence from Letychiv in 1895 about a wedding in Derazhnia with a donation list naming the father, the matchmaker, the groom's brothers and partners in the timber business;
- **articles by local authors** signed with a patronymic and a town;
- **annual indexes** of the volume: a list of correspondents with their towns;
- **subscriber lists** (*prenumeranty*) of Jewish books and newspapers, for example Ha-Maggid and Ha-Melitz — a source of surnames and places when there are no metrical books (Kh. Fridman) [checked].

How to search:
- name and town **in Hebrew and Yiddish**, in several spellings; the newspapers write Russian surnames the Yiddish way;
- limit the search by **years and by publication**;
- recognition of the old typeface makes mistakes — check what matters **against the image**;
- work slowly: if you search often, the site switches on a check (see [Access, captchas, pace](19-access-captchas-pace.md)).

The Russian press is useful too: "Kommersant" (Коммерсантъ) of 1909–1917 printed provincial chronicle with obituaries and trade news, and the "Senatskie ob"yavleniya" (Senate announcements) — auctions, mortgages and summonses to heirs with full names and patronymics. More in [Press](../../catalog/press.md). A step-by-step example — [Case study](../../examples/case-study-newspaper-to-family.md). Don't read Russian? See [For English speakers](../0-start/08-for-english-speakers.md).

## 7. Cemeteries and gravestones

On the Hebrew side of a gravestone there is usually the **father's name** ("ben …" / "bat …"). Photograph both sides of the stone. In emigration a landsmanshaft (a society of people from the same town) bought a plot in a cemetery, and the burial place points to the home town (see [Emigration](13-emigration.md)).

Databases: JOWBR (JewishGen), Mitzvat Emet (מצוות אמת), Toldot, the "Stone Archive" («Каменный архив») on j-roots, Find a Grave — [catalog: cemeteries](../../catalog/cemeteries.md). How to find out whether a cemetery survives, how to read a gravestone and translate the date — [Cemeteries](10-cemeteries.md).

## 8. Pogroms, the Holocaust, memorial books

- **Pogroms of 1917–1922**: Nadia Lipes's database jewishpogroms.info (about 77,777 records with archival references) and the memorial project of the Am HaZikaron institute pogrom.amhazikaron.org (7,929 victims, 118 pages about shtetls; October 2026). They include survivors too: recipients of aid, orphans, refugees. In detail — [Pogroms 1918–1922](11-pogroms-1918-1922.md).
- **The Holocaust**: the central names database of Yad Vashem, USHMM, "Knyha skorboty Ukrainy" (Book of Sorrow of Ukraine), Arolsen Archives.
- **Memorial (yizkor) books** — books about shtetls written by emigrants after the war; translations and indexes on JewishGen.

See [War, repression, siege](12-war-repression-siege.md) and [Dictionaries and encyclopedias](../../reading/dictionaries-and-encyclopedias.md).

## 9. Where to learn and whom to consult

- **JewishGen**: databases, shtetl pages (KehilaLinks), regional divisions (Ukraine Research Division, Bessarabia SIG, Belarus SIG, LitvakSIG), free help with translation (ViewMate), a search for co-researchers (JewishGen Family Finder).
- The order of first steps from the Ukraine Research Division: create an account; find the shtetl in the Communities Database; open its page; write to the "Town Leader"; look at KehilaLinks; join the JewishGen Discussion Group and search its archive; join a local Jewish genealogical society; take part in the Ukraine RD group on Facebook.
- Encyclopedias and dictionaries, institutes, authors, groups — in [../../reading/](../../reading/).

> **How to ask AI**
>
> *For a plain chat, add: "If you can't open a source, say so; mark fonds, villages and dates you name from memory as [from memory, verify]. Answer in English, and give names, places and archive titles in the original script in parentheses." ([why](../1-ai-search/02-prompt-library.md)). The (browser agent) label is for an AI that opens websites itself; the other prompts suit any chat.*
>
> - "The shtetl [name], [uezd, guberniya]. Draw up a checking plan: who it belonged to in different years of the 19th–20th centuries, which country's archives hold documents for each period, whether there was a crown rabbinate there and where its metrical books went — and where I can check each point (a gazetteer, Routes to Roots, the archive's website). Mark whatever you can't check as [from memory, verify]." *Check yourself:* the dates of border changes and the archival references — against a gazetteer or the archive's website.
> - "Draw up a list of search queries in Hebrew and Yiddish, in several spellings, for finding [name, surname] from [town] for [years] in the Historical Jewish Press (NLI)." If the AI is a browser agent, you can ask it to run the search too. *Check yourself:* every hit — against the image of the page; the recognised text makes mistakes.
>
> *Bad → good:* "Where are the metrical books of the Jews of the shtetl [name]?" (no uezd or years, and borders and archives changed) → "The shtetl [name], [uezd, guberniya], [years]: which country's archive holds the documents for these years, and where can I check this?"
