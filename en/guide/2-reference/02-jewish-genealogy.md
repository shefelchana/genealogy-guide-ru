# Jewish genealogy: how things were organised

What this section covers: what you need to know about the life of the Jews of the Russian Empire in order to understand where to look for documents. The Pale of Settlement, communities and rabbis, names, shifting borders, the Jewish press and cemeteries.

---

## 1. The Pale of Settlement and the shtetls

Most Jews of the Russian Empire were allowed to live only in the western guberniyas — in the **Pale of Settlement** (*cherta osedlosti*; present-day Ukraine, Belarus, Lithuania, Moldova, eastern Poland). Life went on in **shtetls** (*mestechki*: small towns with a large Jewish community) and in cities.

For research this means:
- almost all pre-revolutionary documents of a Jewish family lie in the archives of the **guberniyas of the Pale** (Podolia, Kyiv, Volhynia, Kherson, Bessarabia and others);
- if an ancestor lived outside the Pale before 1917 (for example, in Moscow or St Petersburg), look in directories such as "Vsya Moskva" [All Moscow], "Ves Leningrad" [All Leningrad] and similar, in medical directories and student lists — and still check the place of registration in the Pale.

## 2. The community: kahal, "society", crown rabbi

- Until **1844** a Jewish community was governed by the **kahal**; afterwards the community was called a "**society**" (*obshchestvo*). That is why early documents are often grouped "by kahal" (the Polish censuses of the Jews of 1765–1791, for example, are arranged this way).
- From **1835** metrical books were kept by **crown rabbis** (*kazyonnye ravviny*) — appointed by the state, as distinct from the community's spiritual rabbis. So look for the fonds of the shtetls' "rabbinates". Example: the Central State Historical Archive of Ukraine (TsDIAK, Kyiv) holds a list of the surviving rabbinates of Kyiv guberniya with reference codes and years (Berdychiv, Ruzhyn, Skvyra and about 70 others).

### If the family had a "rabbi"

- Family memory also calls a mere learned man without a congregation a "rabbi". Rabbinic families did not include in their pedigrees sons and sons-in-law who did not become scholars (the Center for Jewish History guide "Rabbinical Genealogy") [checked].
- **The prefaces, afterwords and approbations (*haskamot*) of rabbinic books** often set out the author's pedigree and mention other rabbinic families. Secular genealogy usually does not read these books, and religious genealogy does not go to archives; the combination yields results (Am HaZikaron Institute). The books are on HebrewBooks and in the National Library of Israel.
- Reference works: "Ohole Shem" (1912) — rabbis by town; Deych's index "Synagogues and their servants in the Pale of Settlement, 1853–1854" on JewishGen (jewishgen.org/databases/misc/deych.htm) [checked].
- A family pedigree "from a great rabbi" is checked against gravestones and the chronology of the generations ([Cemeteries](10-cemeteries.md)). The pedigrees of famous dynasties are often based on family tradition, not on documents.

## 3. Estates and registration

Estate (*soslovie*) — *meshchane* (townsmen), merchants, agricultural colonists, guild artisans — is a **status, not an origin**. Moving between estates was common. Documents are searched **by place of registration**, not by place of residence. More in [Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](01-records-empire-ussr.md), the section "Estate and registration".

## 4. Names

- Surnames were fixed by the law of 1804, finally by the 1830s.
- Double names (Berko-Duvid) are one name.
- Children were named after **deceased** relatives.
- Documents contain everyday forms (Berko, Srul, Moshko) and Russian ones (Boris, Izrail, Mikhail).

All of this in detail — in [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md).

## 5. Borders changed — and so did the archives

The same shtetl could belong to different states in different years. Then look for documents in the archive of **that** state.

| Territory | What it was | What it means for the search |
|---|---|---|
| **Bessarabia** | from 1812 — Russian Empire | Russian metrical books, revisions (archives of Moldova — ANRM, partly on FamilySearch) |
| **Southern Bessarabia** (Izmail, Kiliya, Bolgrad, Cahul) | **1856–1878 — Romania** (Principality of Moldavia) | someone born there in 1868 is recorded in a **Romanian** civil registration, not a Russian metrical book. Akkerman stayed on the Russian side in those same years |
| **Bessarabia** | 1918–1940 — Romania | Romanian civil registration records |
| **Galicia** | Austria (Austria-Hungary) until 1918 | Austrian metrical books; the databases of JRI-Poland, Gesher Galicia, AGAD |
| **Congress Poland** (*Tsarstvo Polskoe*) | under Russia, but with its own system of vital records | JRI-Poland, Geneteka, Polish archives |
| **Transcarpathia** | Hungary until 1920; Czechoslovakia 1919–1938; Hungary again 1938/39–1944 | see also [Citizenship by descent](14-citizenship-by-descent.md) |

A real example. A man was born in Izmail in 1868. There is no Russian metrical book and there cannot be: the town was then Romanian. Romanian records of Izmail for 1865–1878 are not on FamilySearch; the collection "Romania, Civil Registration 1865–1903" is very small. The guide to the Izmail archive (1966) confirms: there are no materials for 1856–1878. The absence of a record here is a property of the source, not a failure of the search.

## 6. The Jewish press

Newspapers in Hebrew and Yiddish of the 19th and early 20th centuries are among the most underrated sources. The largest collection is the **Historical Jewish Press** of the National Library of Israel (NLI): "Ha-Tsfira", "Ha-Melitz", "Ha-Magid", "Yidishes Folksblat", "Forverts" and others, searchable by recognised text.

What to look for there:
- **advertisements**: searches for missing husbands (a wife abandoned by her husband without a divorce — an *agunah* — could not remarry and looked for him through the newspaper), searches for relatives, weddings, mourning notices;
- **correspondence from the shtetls**: chronicle, scandals, weddings; often signed with a pseudonym;
- **donation lists**: full names, sometimes with patronymic and place of residence. A real example: a 1895 correspondence from Letychiv about a wedding in Derazhnia with a donation list naming the father, the matchmaker, the groom's brothers and partners in the timber business;
- **articles by local authors** with a signature, patronymic and town;
- **annual indexes** of the volume: a list of correspondents with their towns;
- **subscriber lists** (*prenumeranty*) of Jewish books and newspapers, for example of "Ha-Magid" and "Ha-Melitz" — a source of surnames and places when there are no metrical books (H. Fridman) [checked].

How to search:
- the name and town — **in Hebrew and Yiddish**, in several spellings; newspapers write Russian surnames in Yiddish fashion;
- limit the search by **years and publication**;
- recognition of old typefaces makes errors — check the key text **against the image**;
- work slowly: with frequent queries the site switches on a check (see [Access, captchas, pace](19-access-captchas-pace.md)).

The Russian press is useful too: "Kommersant" of 1909–1917 printed provincial chronicle with obituaries and trade news; the "Senatskie ob"yavleniya" [Senate announcements] — auctions, pledges and summonses to heirs with full names and patronymics (Russian-language sources). More in [Press](../../catalog/press.md). A step-by-step example — [Case study: from a newspaper to a family](../../examples/case-study-newspaper-to-family.md).

## 7. Cemeteries and gravestones

On the Hebrew side of a gravestone there is usually the **father's name** ("ben …" / "bat …"). Photograph both sides of the stone. In emigration, a society of countrymen (*landsmanshaft*) bought a plot in a cemetery, and the burial place points to the home town (see [Emigration](13-emigration.md)).

Databases: JOWBR (JewishGen), "Mitzvat Emet", Toldot, the "Stone Archive" (*Kamenny arkhiv*) on j-roots, Find a Grave — [catalog: cemeteries](../../catalog/cemeteries.md). How to find out whether a cemetery survives, how to read a gravestone and convert the date — [Cemeteries](10-cemeteries.md).

## 8. Pogroms, the Holocaust, memorial books

- **Pogroms of 1917–1922**: Nadia Lipes's database jewishpogroms.info (about 77,777 records with archival references) and the memorial project of the Am HaZikaron Institute, pogrom.amhazikaron.org (7,929 victims, 118 pages about shtetls; October 2026). They include survivors too: recipients of aid, orphans, refugees. In detail — [Pogroms 1918–1922](11-pogroms-1918-1922.md).
- **The Holocaust**: the central names database of Yad Vashem, USHMM, "Knyha skorboty Ukrainy" [Book of Sorrow of Ukraine], Arolsen Archives.
- **Memorial books (yizkor)** — books about the shtetls written by emigrants after the war; translations and indexes on JewishGen.

See [War, repression, siege](12-war-repression-siege.md) and [Dictionaries, encyclopedias, reference works](../../reading/dictionaries-and-encyclopedias.md).

## 9. Where to learn and whom to consult

- **JewishGen**: databases, shtetl pages (KehilaLinks), regional divisions (Ukraine Research Division, Bessarabia SIG, Belarus SIG, LitvakSIG), free help with translation (ViewMate), a search for "co-researchers" (JewishGen Family Finder).
- The order of first steps from the Ukraine Research Division: create an account; find the shtetl in the Communities Database; open its page; write to the Town Leader; look at KehilaLinks; join the JewishGen discussion group and search its archive; join a local Jewish genealogical society; take part in the Ukraine RD group on Facebook.
- Encyclopedias and dictionaries, institutes, authors, groups — in [../../reading/](../../reading/).

> **How to ask AI**
>
> - "Shtetl [N], [uezd, guberniya]. Who did it belong to in different years of the 19th–20th centuries, which country's archive holds documents for each period, was there a crown rabbinate there, and where did its metrical books go? Some of the sources may be in Russian, Ukrainian or Romanian — please say which." *Check yourself:* the dates of border changes and archival references — against a reference work or the archive's website.
> - "Find mentions of [given name, surname] from [town] for [years] in the Historical Jewish Press (NLI): draw up a list of search queries in Hebrew and Yiddish in several spellings." *Check yourself:* every hit — against the page image; the recognised text makes errors.
> - "My family says my great-grandfather was a 'rabbi'. How can I check this: which directories of rabbis and synagogue officials, prefaces to books, and subscriber lists of Jewish books should I look at?" *Check yourself:* family memory also calls a merely learned man a "rabbi".
