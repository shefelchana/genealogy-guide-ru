# Handwriting, transliteration, dates

## 1. Russian manuscripts of the 19th century

- **FamilySearch Wiki, "Russia Handwriting"** [checked]. It has:
  - the video course "Reading Russian Handwriting Records" in the Learning Center: letters, column headings of Orthodox, Catholic, **Jewish** and Lutheran metrical books, exercises;
  - the "Field Guide to Russian Letters" — with the letters that have fallen out of use;
  - the Russian Genealogical Word List — a glossary of genealogical words.
- **Pre-reform orthography** (ѣ, ъ, і, ѳ). The reform of 1918 removed several letters (FamilySearch, same place). A manual: S. Vinitsky, "Vvedenie v doreformennuyu orfografiyu" (Introduction to pre-reform orthography; 2006), a PDF on imwerden.de (in Russian) [checked].
- **BYU Script Tutorial** — a section on Russian handwriting (script.byu.edu/russian-handwriting) [from search snippet].
- The compiler did not come across a Russian-language trainer for 19th-century cursive like the BYU one. There are university courses (HSE, RSUH) and a course by I. S. Belyaev on cursive of the 15th–18th centuries [from search snippet].

- **What a surname looks like in cursive.** Steve Morse's tool (stevemorse.org/russian/cyrprintcurs.html) shows a printed word in handwritten letterforms: type in a surname and see what "shape" to look for with your eyes [checked].
- **Browser auto-translation spoils archival terms**: "справа" (file) turns into "affairs", "фонд" into "stock", "опис" into "description", "ревізькі казки" into "tales-revisions". Read file titles in the original (an example from a FamilySearch handout on the files of A. Krakovsky) [checked].

### Letters that get confused

Alexander Beider (Avotaynu, "Some Issues in Ashkenazic Name Searches", 2007) names the pairs that get confused in handwriting: **a/o, g/ch, m/t** (а/о, г/ч, м/т) [checked]. When searching for a surname, try these variants too. Other pairs (B/V, Sh/T, R/K) — in [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md).

### Reading with AI: two passes

AI helpers read a printed form well and handwritten names and places worse. In the first pass give the type of document, the year and the place, but **not the names you expect**; in the second, compare what was read with your hypotheses. Work with a high-resolution fragment. In detail — [Checking results](../1-ai-search/04-checking-results.md).

## 2. Polish and Latin metrical books

**Polish Roots Project, "How to Read Old Polish Records"** [checked]:
- learn **40–60 repeating words** ("born", "united in marriage", "died");
- understand the **order of columns** — it is the same from record to record;
- in the metrical books of the "Russian period" (after 1868) the entries are in Russian. 2–4 hours is enough to learn to recognise the Cyrillic letters;
- keep a **table of correspondences of a name** in Latin, Polish and Russian;
- post a hard record to a group: a native speaker often deciphers it in minutes.

**Jonathan D. Shea and William F. Hoffman, "In Their Words"**: four volumes — Polish, Russian, Latin, German — with analyses of real documents, including revision lists and passports, and templates of letters to archives [from search snippet: pgsa.org].

## 3. German Kurrent (Gothic cursive)

## 4. Transliteration: rules and standards

Don't read Russian? See [For English speakers](../0-start/08-for-english-speakers.md) for a practical guide to Cyrillic–Latin spellings; this section only gives the standards.

### The JewishGen rule: enter it as in the document

JewishGen's transcription rules ("Transcription Guidelines") [checked]:
- there is **no single standard**;
- for Cyrillic — **BGN/PCGN**;
- for Yiddish — **YIVO** (or the Library of Congress);
- for the Kingdom of Poland — Polish Latin spelling;
- **enter the name exactly as in the document**: do not turn Movsha into Moshe or Moses;
- spelling variants are caught by the Daitch–Mokotoff Soundex phonetic search.

### Three systems you will meet

| System | What it is for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **BGN/PCGN** | Russian Cyrillic → Latin; recommended by JewishGen | Хмельницкий → Khmel'nitskiy |
| **YIVO** | Yiddish → Latin | חיים → khayim |
| **ISO 9** | international standard: one Cyrillic letter — one Latin character, with diacritics | Хмельницкий → Hmelʹnickij |

The examples in the table were made up by the general rules of the systems, not taken from a report or from databases. ISO 9 is unambiguous and reversible, but is rare in genealogical databases: BGN/PCGN or "as it sounds" is more common there.

**ALA-LC tables** (Library of Congress) are used by libraries. The FamilySearch Wiki page "Russia Jewish Research" refers to them, and it also has Steve Morse's automatic transliterator [from search snippet]. Tools for transliteration and phonetic search — [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md), section 11.

### A practical conclusion for letters and databases

This is a conclusion from the JewishGen rules, not a quotation:
- in a letter to a foreign archive write the name **as in the document** (in Russian or Yiddish) **and in BGN/PCGN**, with variants in brackets;
- in your own files keep the **original spelling**; keep the transliteration beside it, not instead of it;
- in databases search both for the exact spelling and phonetically.

## 5. Dates: the Julian and Gregorian calendars

- Russia moved to the Gregorian calendar in **1918** (FamilySearch Wiki, "Julian and Gregorian Calendars") [checked]. Before that, documents are dated Old Style.
- The difference is 11 days in the 18th century, 12 days in the 19th century and 13 days from March 1900 (checked 2026-10-07: the difference grows by a day at the end of February 1700, 1800 and 1900 — they are leap years only in the Julian calendar). For the revision of 1795 it is 11 days.
- **In disputed cases write a double date** (FamilySearch, same place): "3/15 March 1875". In documents of the Kingdom of Poland under the empire a double date of the form "23/6 ноября/декабря" (23/6 November/December) occurs [from search snippet: Wikipedia, "Dual dating"].
- In your timeline and findings log write **the date as in the document** and mark the style: "(O.S.)" (*st. st.*) or "(N.S.)" (*n. st.*). Put the conversion beside it, not instead of it.
- **Steve Morse's converters** (stevemorse.org, One-Step): Julian ↔ Gregorian with a choice of country [from search snippet].

## 6. The Jewish calendar: the "plus or minus one day" rule

Avrohom A. Krauss (Avotaynu, "Using the Hebrew Calendar to Solve Date Discrepancies in Genealogical Records", 2010) [checked]:

- **The Jewish day begins in the evening**, and the year in the autumn. So one Jewish date falls on two civil ones. A child born in the evening was, by the Jewish reckoning, born "the next day".
- East European families remembered dates **by the Jewish calendar**: yahrzeit (the anniversary of death), bar mitzvah. Civil dates in questionnaires and emigration documents are therefore often **approximate** — recalculated from memory.
- The method: find the **"kernel of truth"** in the wrong date, restore the Jewish date and recalculate it. A difference of one day (and for dates near the Jewish New Year, of a year) may be explained exactly by this.

Steve Morse's Jewish calendar converter can also work with dates on gravestones (stevemorse.org/jcal/tombstone.html) [checked].

**Two traps from N. Lipes's practice:**
- **A Jewish date taken for a Gregorian one.** The day of a Jewish month passes into a biography with the month of the Gregorian calendar: "25 Sivan" turns into "25 July". "But Sivan is Sivan, not July" (N. Lipes, isrageo.com, 2020). If a "strange" date matches the day of a Jewish month, recalculate it.
- **The date in a boy's birth record** is usually the date of the circumcision; the birth was 7–8 days before it (more in [Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](01-records-empire-ussr.md), section 2).

How to read a date on a gravestone — [Cemeteries](10-cemeteries.md), section 5.

> **How to ask AI**
>
> *For a plain chat, add: "If you can't open a source, say so; mark fonds, villages and dates you name from memory as [from memory, verify]. Answer in English, and give names, places and archive titles in the original script in parentheses." ([why](../1-ai-search/02-prompt-library.md)). The (browser agent) label is for an AI that opens websites itself; the other prompts suit any chat.*
>
> - "This is a scan of [document], [place, year]. In the first pass read it knowing nothing about my hypotheses: the header, then the lines I need verbatim, anything unreadable as '(?)'. In the second pass compare it with what I am looking for: [names]." *Check yourself:* names and figures — against an enlarged fragment; the AI tends to see what is expected.
> - "Convert the date [Jewish date] into the Gregorian and Julian calendars. Take into account Adar I or II, and allow ±1 day (the date may have been counted from the evening)." *Check yourself:* in the Hebcal or Steve Morse converter.
>
> *Bad → good:* "What does this say? [scan]" (no document type, place or year — it reads at random) → "This is a metrical book of [place], [year]: read the header, then line [number] verbatim, anything illegible as (?)."
