## 1. Russian manuscript of the 19th century

- **FamilySearch Wiki, "Russia Handwriting"** [checked]. It has:
  - a video course "Reading Russian Handwriting Records" in the Learning Center: letters, column headings of Orthodox, Catholic, **Jewish** and Lutheran metrical books, exercises;
  - the "Field Guide to Russian Letters" — including letters that have fallen out of use;
  - the Russian Genealogical Word List — a glossary of genealogical words.
- **Pre-reform orthography** (ѣ, ъ, і, ѳ). The reform of 1918 removed several letters (FamilySearch, same page). A manual: S. Vinitsky, "Vvedenie v doreformennuyu orfografiyu" [Introduction to Pre-Reform Orthography] (2006), PDF on imwerden.de [checked] (in Russian).
- **BYU Script Tutorial** — the section on Russian handwriting (script.byu.edu/russian-handwriting) [from search snippet, 403].
- A Russian-language trainer for 19th-century cursive, like BYU's, could not be found. There are university courses (HSE, RSUH) and a course by I. S. Belyaev on cursive of the 15th–18th centuries [from search snippet].

- **How a surname looks in cursive.** Steve Morse's tool (stevemorse.org/russian/cyrprintcurs.html) shows a printed word in handwritten letterforms: type in the surname and see what "shape" to look for with your eyes [checked].
- **Browser auto-translation spoils archival terms**: "справа" (file) turns into "affairs", "фонд" (fond) into "stock", "опис" (inventory) into "description", "ревізькі казки" (revision lists) into "tales-revisions". Read file titles in the original (an example from a FamilySearch handout on the files of A. Krakovsky) [checked].

### Letters that get confused

Alexander Beider (Avotaynu, "Some Issues in Ashkenazic Name Searches", 2007) names the pairs that get confused in manuscripts: **a/o, g/ch, m/t** [checked]. When searching for a surname, run through such variants too. Other pairs (B/V, Sh/T, R/K) — in [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md).

### Reading with AI: two passes

AI assistants read a printed form well and handwritten names and places worse. In the first pass give the type of document, the year and the place, but **not the expected names**; in the second, compare what was read with your hypotheses. Work with a fragment at high resolution. In detail — [Checking results](../1-ai-search/04-checking-results.md).

## 2. Polish and Latin metrical books

**Polish Roots Project, "How to Read Old Polish Records"** [checked]:
- learn **40–60 recurring words** ("was born", "were joined in marriage", "died");
- understand the **order of the columns** — it is the same from record to record;
- in metrical books of the "Russian period" (after 1868) the entries are in Russian. Two to four hours are enough to learn to recognise the Cyrillic letters;
- keep a **table of name equivalents** in Latin, Polish and Russian;
- post a difficult entry to a group: a native speaker often deciphers it in minutes.

**Jonathan Shea and William Hoffman, "In Their Words"** (Jonathan D. Shea, William F. Hoffman): four volumes — Polish, Russian, Latin, German — with analyses of real documents, including revision lists and passports, and templates of letters to archives [from search snippet: pgsa.org].

## 3. German Kurrent (Gothic cursive)

## 4. Transliteration: rules and standards

### The JewishGen rule: enter as in the document

The JewishGen transcription rules ("Transcription Guidelines") [checked]:
- there is **no single standard**;
- for Cyrillic — **BGN/PCGN**;
- for Yiddish — **YIVO** (or the Library of Congress);
- for Congress Poland — Polish Latin spelling;
- **enter the name exactly as in the document**: do not turn Movsha into Moshe or Moses;
- variant spellings are caught by the Daitch-Mokotoff Soundex phonetic search.

### Three systems you will meet

| System | Used for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **BGN/PCGN** | Russian Cyrillic → Latin; recommended by JewishGen | Хмельницкий → Khmel'nitskiy |
| **YIVO** | Yiddish → Latin | חיים → khayim |
| **ISO 9** | international standard: one Cyrillic letter — one Latin character, with diacritics | Хмельницкий → Hmelʹnickij |

The examples in the table were composed by the general rules of the systems, not taken from a report or databases. ISO 9 is unambiguous and reversible, but is rare in genealogical databases: there BGN/PCGN or a spelling "as it sounds" is more common.

**ALA-LC tables** (Library of Congress) are used by libraries. The FamilySearch Wiki page "Russia Jewish Research" refers to them, and it also has Steve Morse's automatic transliterator [from search snippet]. Tools for transliteration and phonetic search — [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md), section 11.

### A practical conclusion for letters and databases

This is a conclusion from the JewishGen rules, not a quotation:
- in a letter to a foreign archive write the name **as in the document** (in Russian or Yiddish) **and in BGN/PCGN**, with variants in brackets;
- in your own files keep the **original spelling**; keep the transliteration beside it, not instead of it;
- in databases search both the exact spelling and phonetically.

## 5. Dates: Julian and Gregorian calendars

- Russia switched to the Gregorian calendar in **1918** (FamilySearch Wiki, "Julian and Gregorian Calendars") [checked]. Before that, documents are dated Old Style.
- The difference is 11 days in the 18th century, 12 days in the 19th century and 13 days from March 1900 (checked 2026-10-07: the difference grows by a day at the end of February of 1700, 1800 and 1900 — they are leap years only in the Julian calendar). For the revision of 1795 it is 11 days.
- **In doubtful cases write a double date** (FamilySearch, same page): "3/15 March 1875". In documents of Congress Poland in imperial times a double date of the form "23/6 November/December" occurs [from search snippet: Wikipedia, "Dual dating"].
- In the timeline and the dossier record the **date as in the document** and mark the style: "(O.S.)" (*st. st.*) or "(N.S.)" (*n. st.*). Put the conversion beside it, not instead of it.
- **Steve Morse's converters** (stevemorse.org, One-Step): Julian ↔ Gregorian with a choice of country [from search snippet].

## 6. The Hebrew calendar: the "plus or minus one day" rule

Avrohom A. Krauss (Avotaynu, "Using the Hebrew Calendar to Solve Date Discrepancies in Genealogical Records", 2010) [checked]:

- **The Jewish day begins in the evening**, and the year in the autumn. So one Hebrew date falls on two civil ones. A child born in the evening was, by the Jewish count, born "the next day".
- Eastern European families remembered dates **by the Hebrew calendar**: *yahrzeit* (anniversary of a death), bar mitzvah. So civil dates in questionnaires and emigration documents are often **approximate** — recalculated from memory.
- The method: find the **"kernel of truth"** in the erroneous date, reconstruct the Hebrew date and convert it again. A discrepancy of one day (and for dates around the Jewish New Year, of a year) may be explained by exactly this.

Steve Morse's Hebrew calendar converter can also work with dates on gravestones (stevemorse.org/jcal/tombstone.html) [checked].

**Two traps from the practice of N. Lipes:**
- **A Hebrew date taken for a Gregorian one.** The day of the Hebrew month passes into a biography with a month of the Gregorian calendar: "25 Sivan" turns into "25 July". "But Sivan is Sivan, not July" (N. Lipes, isrageo.com, 2020). If a "strange" date matches a day of a Hebrew month, convert it.
- **The date in a boy's birth record** is usually the date of circumcision; the birth was 7–8 days before it (more in [Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](01-records-empire-ussr.md), section 2).

How to read the date on a gravestone — [Cemeteries](10-cemeteries.md), section 5.

> **How to ask AI**
>
> - "This is a scan of a [document], [place, year]. In the first pass, read it knowing nothing about my hypotheses: the header, then the lines I need verbatim, marking anything unreadable '(?)'. In the second pass compare with what I am looking for: [names]. The text is in Russian (pre-1918 spelling) / Polish / Latin — please say which language you are reading." *Check yourself:* names and numbers — against an enlarged fragment; AI tends to see what it expects.
> - "Convert the date [Hebrew date] to the Gregorian and Julian calendars. Take into account Adar I or II, and allow plus or minus 1 day (the date may have been counted from the evening)." *Check yourself:* in the Hebcal or Steve Morse converter.
> - "A biography says the person was born on [day, month]. Could this be a day of a Hebrew month mistaken for a Gregorian one?" *Check yourself:* compare with the Hebrew date in the metrical record.
> - "Transliterate [name, place] by the rules of BGN/PCGN, ISO 9 and (for Yiddish forms) YIVO, and show how it might be written in English-language databases." *Check yourself:* in the JewishGen databases enter it as in the document.
