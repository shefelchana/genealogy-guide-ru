Requests to Archives and Letters to People

What this section covers: how to write a request to an archive so that it is understood and fulfilled, what is known about procedure in different countries, and how to write to strangers — DNA matches, people with the same surname, authors of trees, volunteers. The request template is the archive request template.

The markers in square brackets ([checked], [from search snippet], [unverified] and others) are explained in the Glossary. Here "[from search snippet]" means that the official page could not be opened: check the procedure on the archive's website before sending. Prices and deadlines as of October 2026.

Rule of this guide. A letter to an archive comes after the online routes have been tried and recorded in the research log; it is targeted, with an exact archival reference (fond–inventory–file); the decision and the sending are the person's. An AI helper can help draft the text if you ask it to, but does not itself suggest a letter instead of searching.

1. First — check whether it is online

Almost all outside guides (FamilySearch Letter Writing Guide, VGD "Advice for beginners" (Russian-language), guides to Polish archives) name a written request as one of the main steps. But a great deal has already been digitised: Ukrainian metrical books and revision lists on Wikimedia Commons, Moldovan ones on FamilySearch, Polish ones in JRI-Poland, Geneteka and szukajwarchiwach. Before you write and pay, check Archives by country and ../../catalog/.

A request makes sense when:

  • the file certainly exists but is not digitised;
  • you need an official archival certificate or a certified copy (for example, for citizenship — Citizenship by descent);
  • the document is closed to free access and issued only to relatives (Soviet documents younger than 75 years, files of the repressed).

Where a ZAGS record is: the term in the law is not yet the fact of transfer [reader's experience]. Civil registration books are kept at the registry office (ZAGS) for a term set by law — for the author 75 years; it differs between countries and over time — and are then transferred to the state archive. Hence the temptation: the event is older than this term, so the record is already in the archive. For the author, in one city it turned out otherwise. The archive had in fact accepted books only up to 1936 and had itself explained this publicly by a lack of storage space. The archive's catalogue had exactly one fond of registry offices — 1,019 files, all within 1919–1936 — while the fond's card said 1919–1934, lagging behind even its own files. The gap between the term in the law and the fact was almost twenty years.

→ Check not the law but the fond: whether the archive's catalogue has a fond of registry offices, what its outer dates are by the files themselves (not only by the fond's card), how many units it has. This is one query to the catalogue, and it shows where the record really is. If the books have not been transferred, the record stays at the registry office; there, information about other people is usually issued only to relatives who prove the relationship, and the relative applies for it themselves.

⚠️ Paid "genealogical searches" from private intermediaries usually name the archive and the file number rather than supply the documents themselves. Before paying, check whether it is already freely available.

2. How to write a request to an archive

The general outline (FamilySearch, "Letter Writing Guide for Genealogy" and "Russian Letter Writing Guide"):

  1. the date;
  2. to whom (the director of the archive) and the address;
  3. a salutation;
  4. who you are;
  5. information about the ancestor: full name (in the spelling of the period and in the modern one), dates, place (shtetl, uezd, guberniya), religion, estate (soslovie);
  6. one short, specific question;
  7. a request to suggest where to apply if they do not have it;
  8. a sentence about payment (willingness to pay, or a question about the cost);
  9. a closing, signature, return address including the country.

Main advice:

  • Short and simple. Do not ask for too much at once.
  • Give the archival reference (fond–inventory–file–folio) if known: that way the archive looks in the specific file and not in the whole archive. A targeted request ("a copy of record No. 149 on folio …") is handled faster and more cheaply than a thematic one ("find everything about the family").
  • Write in the language of the archive's country (Ukrainian, Romanian, Polish, Russian…); English as a fallback. For Poland — in Polish, from ready-made templates, not by machine translation.
  • The Russian guide on FamilySearch has ready-made phrases in Russian ("was born" in the masculine and feminine, "about a marriage", "about a death", "about his/her parents").

Nadia Lipes advises working with pre-revolutionary documents through local genealogists — and being ready for it to take months.

What else practitioners advise (a retelling; check against the rules of the specific archive before sending):

  • An archive will usually find a specific metrical record if the full names are given roughly correctly and the date is not off by more than a couple of years. Side branches (brothers, cousins) the archive will not search for on its own in response to a request — they must be requested separately (N. Lipes, nadialipes.info).
  • For the Soviet period (ZAGS — civil registry, factories, party organisations, military enlistment offices) an individual can obtain the same amount of information as a professional — by an e-mail request, often free, but slower (same source).
  • An archive's "refusal" often means not the absence of the document but a different birth date, a different place, or a Jewish name instead of the Russian one the family knows. Word the request with variants (genealogist Z. Shklyar in an isrageo.com article, 2020).
  • If a woman changed her surname and first name several times and there are no direct documents, search through her husbands — their personnel files and questionnaires, in which they wrote about their wife (V. Paley, same source).
  • Before ordering from the archive of Moldova, gather all the places (birth, marriage, real estate) the family came from, names in their original form (Abram, not Abraham; Srul, not Israel), the occupation as precisely as possible, and information about deportation or arrest (A. Chastina, IAJGS 2012) [from a retelling, unverified].
  • ZAGS issues a certificate or an extract, but almost never a copy of the register entry itself; a copy can be obtained after the books have been transferred to the state archive (isrageo.com article, 2020).

3. By country

Ukraine

  • The State Archival Service maintains a portal with links to the oblast archives; each archive has its own procedure (FamilySearch).
  • Example: at the State Archive of Ternopil Oblast a genealogical certificate is a paid service: a written application → a contract → payment on completion; the tariffs are approved by order of the director and are not published on the page [checked].
  • [from search snippet] The Chernivtsi and Zhytomyr archives: requests are accepted by post, by e-mail or in person; the cost depends on complexity; state the region, village or town, surname (for women — the maiden name), dates, religion, estate.
  • The language is Ukrainian (not verified against the page).
  • How to write a request to a Ukrainian archive by exact references (online checks, price-list logic, paying from abroad, a template in Ukrainian, a case-list table) — skill requesting-records-from-ukrainian-archives.

Russia

  • According to FamilySearch, Rosarkhiv (the Federal Archival Agency) and many Russian archives are not set up for "family history" requests; they recommend specialised paid services (for example BLITZ, St. Petersburg, preliminary search — $80 per FamilySearch).
  • ZAGS documents are kept in the regions.
  • ZAGS records in Russia [from search results, November 2026]: from 01.01.2025 the state fee for a repeat certificate is 500 ₽, for a certificate-extract (spravka) from a record 350 ₽ (Tax Code of the Russian Federation, art. 333.26). A repeat certificate for a deceased person is issued to a relative or another interested person (Federal Law 143-FZ "On acts of civil status", art. 9). Register books are kept by ZAGS offices for 100 years and then transferred to the state archive: a record older than a hundred years is in the archive, not at ZAGS.
  • Some oblast archives offer paid remote viewing of digitised files (EAIS — unified archive information system) — sometimes this is cheaper and faster than a request. First check the open PDF inventories and name indexes.

Belarus

  • NIAB — the National Historical Archives of Belarus [from search snippet]: genealogical search with a deposit of 360 BYN and a full cost of up to 1,800 BYN; biographical requests for dates of birth, marriage, death; you can also work independently in the reading room, ordering files by archival reference. The Grodno branch accepts requests through its own site.

Moldova

  • [from search snippet] Copies of documents on birth, marriage, death for 1820–1992 can be ordered online through the government services and arhiva.gov.md. Certificates from metrical books of 1820–1924 start from 58 lei, requests by e-mail are no longer accepted, only through servicii.gov.md [checked: arhiva.gov.md/servicii, 2026-10-07] (see Citizenship by descent).
  • In the reading room of the National Archive, work with digitised metrical books is free, while copies and certificates are paid [from search snippet].

Poland

  • First check online: Szukajwarchiwach, Geneteka, PRADZIAD.
  • Write in Polish, from templates (FamilySearch, PGSA).
  • Prices are from $1 to $30 per search; payment is by international money transfer, not by cheque or card. The reply usually comes 1–2 weeks after payment, with scans by e-mail. The question must be clearly limited by years.
  • Source: fromshepherdsandshoemakers.com/2016/06/26/writing-to-archives-in-poland/

Israel

  • The State Archives — Mandate-period immigration and naturalization files, rabbinical courts 1919–2006 (marriages and divorces).
  • The Central Zionist Archives — a paid genealogical service.
  • CAHJP (Central Archives for the History of the Jewish People) at the Hebrew University / the National Library.
  • Birth and death certificates are issued by the Population and Immigration Authority, marriage certificates by the Ministry of Religious Services. Contacts are on the FamilySearch Wiki page "Israel Archives and Libraries".

USA

  • USCIS Genealogy Program — files of deceased immigrants: A-Files, AR-2 (1940–1944), C-Files (1906–1956), Registry Files, Visa Files (1924–1944).
  • Two steps: a search of the index, then an order of copies by file number. Submitted through the portal genealogy.uscis.dhs.gov.
  • Fee: $30 online and $80 on paper for each step [checked: USCIS fee table G-1055, rev. 10/01/26, checked 2026-10-07].
  • A-files transferred to NARA (100 years after birth) are ordered from NARA — search the NARA Catalog first.
  • Source: uscis.gov/records/genealogy.

Germany

  • Arolsen Archives — an inquiry about a person can be submitted free of charge, including in Russian; Russian-speaking staff reply [checked: press release].

4. Letters to strangers

Whom you write to: DNA matches, people with the same surname, authors of other people's trees, researchers of the same shtetls, cemetery volunteers.

Briefly. Who you are, what you are looking for, how to contact you (Watershed DNA). In the first letter — one or two surnames, a place, a generation; details later. Do not overload it with centimorgans and segments: many people do not understand these terms [from search snippet: several blogs]. The subject line, for example: "DNA match on MyHeritage". If the person has several kits, name the kit.

Offer something of your own. The earliest known ancestor with a place, a pedigree chart, your finds, joint work (the FamilyTreeDNA blog). It is better to write when you have something to offer in return. In one study, of four letters to researchers of the same surnames and places, one grew into a correspondence with new information — what helped was having something to give in return (a census read name by name that was not in the open indexes).

Tone. Friendly, conversational, without pressure. Ask them to reply even if the person knows nothing. If they do not reply or decline — accept it and switch to others. In delicate cases (for example, an unknown biological parent) say plainly that you do not want to disrupt anyone's family; give several ways to reply (Watershed DNA). "No one has a right to a relationship" (FamilySearch).

Privacy. Do not forward other people's information and match lists without consent (the BCG code).

A local researcher or historian

A village or a surname often has its own researcher: a local historian, the author of a book about the village, a forum member with a database for the parish. Such a person often has a lineage list (rospis) going back many generations.

  • Ask not for a retelling but for a lineage list with a source for every entry (archive, fond, opis, delo, folio, year), best as GEDCOM or a spreadsheet, plus photos of the folios with your people. Offer your own list of archival references and finds in return.
  • Prepare before the conversation: what is already known (with levels of evidence), three to five concrete questions and a short glossary (lineage list, archival reference, generation, revision, odnodvortsy) so you speak the same language.
  • Record everything said without a source as "according to , " and check it. Do not move information about living people from someone else's list into your tree without their consent.

A sample first letter to a DNA match

Hello! We match on DNA at [service]. I am looking for relatives on the [surname] line from [shtetl, guberniya], late 19th century. The earliest known ancestor is [name], around [year]. If you have ancestors from those places or recognise the surname, I would be glad of a reply, even a short one. I can share a chart of my branch. [Name, how to contact]

More samples: watersheddna.com/blog/draft-language-for-reaching-out-to-a-dna-match; blog.familytreedna.com/contacting-dna-matches-strategies/.

JewishGen Family Finder (JGFF)

  • You enter your surnames and shtetls and see who is searching for the same. Free JewishGen registration is required.
  • Enter all places and surnames in your profile. If only one place is given, a relative from another shtetl will not find you. JGFF itself sends out notifications of matches.
  • Every researcher has a contact button: the letter goes through the site, and the address is not revealed. Check that the letter has actually gone (the page should show the confirmation "Your message was forwarded").

Volunteers

  • Find a Grave: a memorial has a button to request a photograph of the gravestone. Permission to reuse a photo is obtained from the volunteer who took it, not from the site [from search snippet].
  • ViewMate (JewishGen): you post a scan or photo — volunteers help translate or read it for free (Yiddish, Hebrew, Russian). The image stays up for 7 days, then goes to the archive together with the replies. Professionals offering paid services are forbidden to use it [from search snippet].
  • Town Leaders of the JewishGen Ukraine Research Division — curators of the shtetl pages.
  • Forums and groups — Groups and communities.

How to ask AI

For a plain chat, add: "If you can't open a source, say so; mark fonds, villages and dates you name from memory as [from memory, verify]. Answer in English, and give names, places and archive titles in the original script in parentheses." (why). The note "(browsing agent)" is for an AI that opens websites itself; the other prompts suit any chat.

  • "Help me draft a request to [archive] in [language]: what I am looking for, the exact archival reference (fond, inventory, file), all forms of the names, years with a margin, and what I have already checked online." Check yourself: you send the letter; the address and procedure — per the archive's website.
  • "Here is the archive's reply [text]. What does it mean: that the document does not exist, or that they searched wrongly (a different date, a different name)? What should I clarify?" Check yourself: a "refusal" often means a different date, place or Jewish name instead of the Russian one.

Bad → good: "Write to the archive, find everything about the family" (a thematic request without an archival reference is slower and dearer than a targeted one) → "Help me draft a request to [archive]: a copy of record [number] in the file [fond, inventory, file, folio], [name in all its forms]."
