# Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR

What this section covers: which documents exist, what to look for in each, how to descend several generations through revision lists, and why records are found by place of registration, not place of residence.

---

## 1. How an archival reference works

A document in an archive is addressed by three numbers: **fond — inventory (*opis*) — file (*delo*)** (sometimes also a folio). For example, "DAKhmO 226-79-5224" means the State Archive of Khmelnytskyi Oblast (*Derzhavnyi arkhiv Khmelnytskoi oblasti*), fond 226, inventory 79, file 5224.

- **Fond** — everything that survives from one institution (a guberniya administration, a crown rabbi, a chancery).
- **Inventory (*opis*)** — the list of the fond's files with their titles.
- **File (*delo*)** — one folder or book.
- **Folio (*list*)** — a page of the file; there is a "folio" (recto) and a "verso" (*oborot*).

Keep five different things apart:

| What | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **Inventory (*opis*)** | the title of a file, but not its content |
| **Index** (a name index to a fond or a file) | the fact that a surname is in the file |
| **Index** (a database, e.g. JewishGen) | an extract from the record, with errors |
| **Scan** | the document itself |
| **Your transcription** | your own reading of the scan |

Only the scan and a careful reading of it count as a find. The rest are hints about where to look.

## 2. Metrical books

Records of births, marriages, divorces and deaths.
- For Orthodox Christians they were kept by parishes; for Jews, by **crown rabbis** (*kazyonnye ravviny*, from 1835).
- The books were kept in **two copies**: one stayed with the rabbi or the parish, the other went to the guberniya administration or the consistory. If one copy has not survived, look for the other.
- Entries usually give the parents' names, their estate, and the father's place of registration ("*meshchanin* of such-and-such shtetl"); marriage records give the ages and the fathers of the groom and bride. To understand what is in which column, first read the printed header of the table.
- Detailed records sometimes include witnesses who signed in Hebrew — this is the family's circle. In one study a "newborn's uncle" was found among the witnesses.
- **The date in a boy's birth record** is, according to N. Lipes, usually the date of the circumcision; a separate birth date was written only if the circumcision was not on the eighth day. So he was most likely born 7–8 days earlier, and a one-week difference from the family's date is not a contradiction (nadialipes.info — Russian-language site) [not verified as a general rule].
- **Look at all the surviving years**, not just the year the family gave. This is how siblings who died in infancy, cousins and the year of a move are found; the record of an infant's death is sometimes the only document that dates the parents' marriage (N. Lipes).
- A marriage record may be absent because the couple married **in another town** or with another rabbi. Look in the books of neighbouring rabbinates.

Where to look: for Ukraine, what survives is listed in the "Zvedenyi katalog metrychnykh knyh" [Consolidated Catalogue of Metrical Books] (10 volumes in 15 books, 2009–2021, with text search; Lviv Oblast is not in volumes 1–10); for Moldova, FamilySearch microfilms; for Poland, JRI-Poland and Geneteka. In detail — [Archives by country](07-archives-by-country.md) and [Archive file scans](../../catalog/archive-file-scans.md).

## 3. Revision lists (*revizskie skazki*) — the "ladder" of generations

Revisions were censuses of the tax-paying population, taken for taxes and conscription. They let you descend several generations.

| Revision | Year | What matters |
|---|---|---|
| 5th | **1795** | the first after the partitions of Poland–Lithuania; for the Jews of Right-Bank Ukraine, the **earliest**; surnames are often absent |
| 6th | 1811 | **men only** — the absence of a wife or daughter means nothing |
| 7th | **1816** | the first with women; almost everyone has a surname |
| 8th | 1834 | complete |
| 9th | **1850** | complete |
| 10th | **1858** | the last; the family lists (*posemeinye spiski*) of the 1870s refer back to it |

Earlier than 1795, for the Jewish communities of the Right Bank, there are almost only community record books (*pinkasim*) and the tax registers of the *kahals*, with very few survivals. The Polish censuses of the Jews of 1765–1791 have been published (I. Kamanin, "Arkhiv Yugo-Zapadnoi Rossii" [Archive of South-Western Russia]), but they hold only a count of souls and households by *kahal*, with **no lists of names**.

### The bridge between steps

A revision list has a column **"po poslednei revizii sostoyalo — leta"** ["according to the last revision was — years"]: the age of the same person in the previous revision. This is the bridge between generations.

Descend **strictly step by step, never skipping a step**: you found a household in 1858 → you copied out the age from the previous revision → you look for the same household in 1850 at that age → from there to 1834.

The second important column is **"iz togo chisla vybylo — kogda imenno"** ["of that number departed — when exactly"]: "umer v 18.. godu" [died in the year 18..], "otdan v rekruty" [given up as a recruit], "perechislen v …" [transferred to …]. The year of death of the head of a household is often the only available date for a whole generation.

⚠️ Traps:
- **the family number is not preserved between revisions** — link households only by names and ages;
- "бѣжалъ" [fled] in the departure column looks similar to "умеръ" [died] — look carefully;
- household numbers in double-page spreads partly disappear into the binding ("290" reads as "190") — check against neighbouring pages;
- folios are sometimes torn out of files — check the numbering.

For detailed method, see the skill `tracing-revision-records` in [../../../skills/](../../../skills/).

## 4. Estate and registration

Every inhabitant of the empire was **registered** (*pripisan*) to an estate (*soslovie*) and a community: a *meshchanin* (townsman) of such-and-such shtetl, a merchant of such-and-such guild, an agricultural colonist of a colony.

- **Estate is a status, not an origin.** Moving between *meshchane* and merchants, or between guild artisans (*tsekhovye*) and *meshchane*, was common. If your family is known under one estate, check the neighbouring lists too.
- **Registration is not residence.** A person could live in Kyiv or Odesa for decades yet be listed as a *meshchanin* of a shtetl hundreds of versts away. Revisions, draft lists and many other documents were compiled **by place of registration**. The entry "Рашковскій мещ." means "*meshchanin* of the shtetl Rashkov", not a surname.
- A transfer to another community was formalised by a file "on transfer" (*o perechislenii*) or "on registration" (*o prichislenii*). A son who has transferred to another community no longer appears in his father's household — this is normal.
- There are whole series of files "on registration in the tax-paying estates" (for example, in one archive, 1312 files for 1846–1851) that are absent from the ordinary indexes.

## 5. Other pre-revolutionary documents

| Document | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **Draft (recruit) lists** | men in order of the draw, with age; some give the family number from the revision |
| **Censuses**: local ones and the 1897 All-Russian Census | family composition, age, place of registration; a census shows where people lived, not where they were registered. The local copies of the 1897 census were supposed to be destroyed, and only a few districts survive — absence from it means almost nothing (lipesdatabase.com) |
| **Jewish military census of 1875** | age by documents, and where in doubt "by appearance" (there was a separate list of such cases); family number from the previous revision; family members living apart — who, where and by what document; a separate list of vagrants without passports (lipesdatabase.com). A bridge to the 1858 revision |
| **Family lists** (*posemeinye spiski*) of the 1870s | families with a reference to the 10th revision |
| **State Duma voter lists** (1906–1907) | adult men by place; indexed on JewishGen |
| **Crown rabbi voter lists**, "public resolutions" (*obshchestvennye prigovory*) on elections | adult men of the community; sometimes the only place where a family turned up under another spelling of the surname (an example in N. Lipes — Uman) |
| **Police files** of the guberniya administration: passports, expulsions, right of residence, vagrancy | addresses, moves, relatives. According to N. Lipes, police documents survived better than all the others, and a family can sometimes be reconstructed only from them |
| **Notarial and tax documents**, lists of house owners | property, leases, powers of attorney, residence |
| **Court files** | lawsuits, debts, partners; the title in the inventory often contains names and sums |
| **Student files** | birth certificate, certificate of registration, father's name |
| **Directories**: "Vsya Rossiya" [All Russia], address calendars, memorial books of the guberniyas, the "Rossiiskii meditsinskii spisok" [Russian Medical Directory] | profession, place of service, address, year by year |
| **Press**: Jewish (Hebrew, Yiddish) and Russian | advertisements, correspondence from the shtetls, donation lists, obituaries, auctions, summonses to heirs ("Senatskie ob"yavleniya" [Senate announcements]) |

Directories and press — [Directories and books](../../catalog/directories-and-books.md), [Press](../../catalog/press.md).

### If there are no metrical books: a chain of substitutes

1. **The second copy** of the metrical book (with the rabbi or in the guberniya administration).
2. **Books of neighbouring rabbinates and of the district town**: entries from a shtetl are sometimes bound into other books. JRI-Poland, for example, found the long-lost metrical records of one shtetl inside the books of a neighbouring town [from search snippet: Avotaynu, 2018].
3. **The fond of the *zemskii sud*** (rural district court). When there was no crown rabbi, the metrical books were handed over to the *zemskii sud* — sometimes **of another district** (N. Lipes, isrageo.com, 2017).
4. **Censuses**: guberniya, then municipal.
5. **Police files**, recruitment, tax and notarial lists, petitions, school and student files.
6. **Directories and press.**

Children of one family could be born in different guberniyas, and their birth records lie where they were born — even if the family remembers that they "never went anywhere" (N. Lipes).

A useful question for any source: **under what law was it created?** — this explains who is in it, who is not, and which columns to trust (the approach of Judy Russell, The Legal Genealogist). For the Jews of the empire: surnames — the law of 1804; conscription — from 1827; metrical books kept by rabbis — from 1835; the military census of 1874–1875 before universal conscription. A person's absence from a source is often explained by the law, not by loss.

## 6. The Soviet period

- **ZAGS** (the Soviet civil registry office; from 1919–1920): records of births, marriages and deaths. Look at the alphabetical indexes of **all** the surviving years, not just the "right" one. Early records usually have no column for nationality — it appeared in the form of 1926. From 1928 to roughly 1933, according to N. Lipes, parents were not asked for documents when a child was registered: a mother could be recorded under a new, "Russian" name; later the discrepancy was legalised by "same person" (*on zhe*) certificates or through a court (for example, for the sake of an inheritance), and these documents are worth looking for. Some books of 1919–1944 for Ukraine are on Wikimedia Commons (Proskurov, Kyiv, rural districts of Khmelnytskyi Oblast). They have no surname search; some forms (for example, Proskurov 1924–1935, Kyiv 1922) give neither parents nor place of birth.
- **Household books** (*pokhozyaistvennye knigi*) (some regional archives have cross-fond indexes to them).
- **Employment and school files**, personal files of students.
- **Military documents**: registration cards, casualty reports, hospital registers (with a column "address of relatives"), award lists, documents about captivity.
- **Evacuation**: cards of the Central Information Bureau and local bureaus (1941–1942).
- **Repression**: investigation files (NKVD/KGB case files), memorial books (books of remembrance).
- **Holocaust**: witness testimony pages, lists of victims.

How and where to search — [War, repression, siege](12-war-repression-siege.md). For a detailed guide to Soviet documents, see Nadia Lipes's book "Sovetskie dokumenty. Chto, gde, zachem" [Soviet Documents. What, Where, Why] (2018), in [Genealogists and authors](../../reading/genealogists-and-authors.md) (the book is in Russian).

## 7. Who may obtain documents

- Pre-revolutionary metrical books, revision lists, censuses and family lists are available to anyone, **without proof of kinship**.
- Documents with personal data in Russia and Ukraine are closed for **75 years** ("personal and family secrecy"). Before the term expires, access is usually granted on proof of kinship. The exception is Ukraine: the files of the repressive organs are open under a law of 2015 (more in [War, repression, siege](12-war-repression-siege.md)).
- For the procedure at a specific archive, see its website.

## 8. How to work through a shtetl: the order

1. **Routes to Roots** or M. Weiner's book *Jewish Roots in Ukraine and Moldova* — what should have survived for the shtetl.
2. **"Zvedenyi katalog metrychnykh knyh"** [Consolidated Catalogue of Metrical Books] — what survives and in which archive (for Ukraine).
3. **The register of inventories or an inventory** on the archive's website or on Commons — which files exist, what is digitised.
4. **JewishGen and FamilySearch** — is there an index; the index first, then continuous reading.
5. **Commons, FamilySearch microfilms** — the scan itself. Check every match from an index against the scan.
6. **Directories and press** — business, addresses, doctors, advertisements.
7. If the file is not digitised, trace the reference to an **exact archival reference** (fond–inventory–file) and only then write a request (see [Requests and letters](20-requests-letters.md)).

> **How to ask AI**
>
> - "Here is a scan of a [revision list / metrical book], [place], [year]. The source is in Russian (pre-1918 spelling) — please read it as Russian. First read the printed header: what goes in which column. Then copy out household [number] in full: names, age now and 'according to the last revision', those who departed with the year and reason — verbatim, in the original pre-reform orthography." *Check yourself:* the age "according to the last revision" is not confused with the current age; "бѣжалъ" [fled] has not been read as "умеръ" [died].
> - "Here is a household from the [year] revision: [extract]. Calculate what age these people should be in the [previous year] revision and build a table for searching by the age bridge." *Check yourself:* search strictly step by step, never skipping a revision; the family number does not carry over.
> - "There are no metrical books for shtetl [N] for [years]. What documents could replace them (the second copy, neighbouring rabbinates, the rural district court, censuses, police files) and where should they be held?" *Check yourself:* the existence of the fonds — from the archive's inventory, not from the AI's memory.
> - "Here is the line '[text]' from a document (Russian, pre-1918 spelling). What is the given name, patronymic, surname, estate and place of registration here?" *Check yourself:* make sure the registration note ("…ский мещ.") has not been taken for a surname.
