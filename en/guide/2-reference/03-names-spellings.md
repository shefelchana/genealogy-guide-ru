# Names, surnames, spellings

What this section covers: why one person is called differently in different documents, and how to search so that you do not miss your own people. It matters most for Jewish genealogy, but much of it is true for everyone.

> Transliteration between Cyrillic and Latin letters is covered on the page [For English speakers](../0-start/08-for-english-speakers.md); the examples below keep the original Cyrillic spellings so that you can see what the variants look like.

---

## 1. Surnames did not appear at once

For the Jews of the Russian Empire, surnames were fixed by the law of 1804 and finally settled by the 1830s. Earlier there are often no surnames at all, and a "surname" in a document can be a nickname, a trade or the name of a place.

What follows from this:
- before the 1820s search by **given name + patronymic + age + community**, not by surname;
- within one family, brothers could be recorded **under different surnames**. A real example: in the census of 1811 for the shtetl Dunaivtsi, three full brothers, sons of one father, are recorded under three different nicknames — Abramko Shefel (Абрамко Шефель), Aron Gruz (Аронъ Грузъ), Mendel Krotnik (Мендель Кротникъ);
- in revision lists the family number does not carry over from one revision to the next — generations have to be linked by ages (see [Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](01-records-empire-ussr.md)).

## 2. One surname, many spellings

The same surname was written by clerks in Russian, Polish, Romanian and German; people themselves wrote it in Yiddish and Hebrew; in emigration, in English. Add the reading errors of indexers.

Real examples:
- **Shefel (Шефель)** turned up as Шафель, Шеффель, Шефаль, Шейфель, Sheffel, Schefel, Scheffel, Schaffel, Szefel, Shafel, Sofiel — about 20 variants in all. In the JewishGen indexes it is almost nowhere recorded as SHEFEL;
- **Vainberg (Вайнберг)**: Вейнберг, Wainberg, Weinberg, Vainberg, Vainbarg (in an Estonian catalogue). A printed list of students had *Wainberg*, while text recognition gave "Weinberg"; in the index the "Wai-" and "Wei-" blocks ended up on different pages.

What to do:
1. Make a **list of all the variants** before you start searching, and add to it as you go.
2. In databases start with **"exact"** mode, then add **phonetic** search. The difference can be huge: one surname has 122 exact records against 2,293 phonetic ones.
3. Remember that phonetics lies too: the query "Kessel" returned Kishel (Кишель) and Kisel (Кисель), not Kesel (Кесель).
4. Check readings against dictionaries: if a surname is not in Beider's dictionaries, it is almost certainly a misreading. The index of Beider's dictionaries is free on stevemorse.org (see [Dictionaries and encyclopedias](../../reading/dictionaries-and-encyclopedias.md)).
5. **Try different root vowels**: Plet — Plit — Plat — Plot (Плет — Плит — Плат — Плот). This is how N. Lipes found a family in the crown rabbi's voter lists under a form that was not in the database for the uezd (isrageo.com, 2021). B/V/P/F and vowels are interchangeable in old records.
6. Judge the **range of a surname** by the earliest lists that have surnames — 1805–1816 — not by the voter lists of 1906–1907 on which the geography of Beider's dictionary rests: by the 1900s families had scattered (N. Lipes).

⚠️ Similar surnames are not necessarily variants of one. "Шефтманъ" (Sheftman) next to "Шефелемъ" (Shefel) in one shtetl is a separate surname with a different root. Check against a dictionary, not by similarity to the eye.

## 3. Place names are spelled in different ways too

- Transliteration decides: on JewishGen the query "Kamenets-Podolsk" gave 0, "Kamenets" gave 8.
- In American manifests towns are distorted beyond recognition: Pokotilov (Покотилов) is written as "Pocolifoff", Proskurov (Проскуров) as "Proskaw".
- Catalogues give the name in different cases and different languages: "Білилівка / Белиловка" (Bilylivka / Belilovka).
- In Hebrew and Yiddish a town has several spellings: Izmail — איזמאיל and איזמאהיל.

Check distorted names against the JewishGen Gazetteer and the Communities Database (about 2.4 million places; more than 6,000 communities with name variants and Yiddish synonyms).

## 4. Recognised text (OCR) makes predictable mistakes

Recognition software for old books:
- **"eats" or replaces the first letter**: Вайнбергъ turns into "Байнбергъ" and "айнбергь". Search **without the first letter**: "ефель", "айнберг";
- confuses similar letters, and in Hebrew the final and ordinary forms (ב read as כ);
- mixes up columns in directories: neighbouring lines in the text are not necessarily neighbours on the page.

That is why a zero in full-text search proves nothing by itself (see "control record" in [Standard of proof](../3-results/01-standard-of-proof.md)).

Russian directories are recognised in **Cyrillic**. On Genealogy Indexer "Shefel" in Latin letters gave 1 match in the whole database, "Шефель" — 15, "Шеффель" — 92. FamilySearch also says that for the Soviet military databases the Russian spelling gives noticeably more than transliteration.

Try **both orthographies** — pre-reform and modern. A Google Books search for the phrase "Вайнбергъ Моисей" (with a hard sign at the end) found a directory that had not turned up for two weeks with an ordinary query. Yandex "Search in archives" (Яндекс «Поиск по архивам») understands pre-reform spelling and cases by itself.

## 5. Handwriting: which letters get confused

In 19th-century manuscripts B and V, Sh and T, the endings "-ель" and "-еръ", R and K are often confused. How to check:
- compare how **the same clerk** writes these letters in neighbouring entries;
- enlarge the word you need rather than reading the whole sheet at once;
- do not fit the reading to your expectation. A real case: "Михель Кр…торъ" was wanted as "Репиторъ", but at higher magnification turned out to be "Кройторъ".

## 6. Jewish names: double, everyday, Russified

**Double names** (Berko-Duvid, Khaim-Iosi, Meishe-Leiba) are one name. A clerk might write only half. In the Kishinev marriage index you can see that "Берко-Дувид" is a double name, not "Berko, son of Duvid": for other grooms the patronymics there are written with the ending "-овъ".

**One name in different forms.** Useful correspondences:

| In the document | Full Jewish name |
|---|---|
| Berko (Берко) | Dov-Ber |
| Srul (Сруль) | Yisrael |
| Moshko, Movsha (Мошко, Мовша) | Moshe |
| Leizer, Leizor (Лейзер, Лейзор) | Eliezer |

**A Russian form beside the Jewish one** is common in Soviet documents: Gersh Shlyumovich (Герш Шлюмович) = Grigory Solomonovich; Movsha = Mikhail; Khaya = Klara; Berko = Boris; Srul = Izrail. The Soviet "Rossiiskii meditsinskii spisok" (Russian Medical Directory) of 1924 often gives both forms side by side.

**Russifications are tendencies, not rules.** N. Lipes gives a long list of non-obvious pairs (nadialipes.info, the article "Names" — in Russian): Leib (Arye-Leib) → Lev **or Aleksei**; Akiva → Kiva → Nikolai; Avigdor → Viktor; Nisan → Andrei; Motl → Matvei or Dmitri; Efraim, Faivish → Fyodor or Filipp; Usher → Yuri or Georgi; Kopel → Yakov; Ber, Berko, Bentsion, Benyamin → Boris; Issakhar → Aleksandr. Female: Shneidl → Evgenia; Zisl, Zeidl → Zoya or Zinaida; Golda ↔ Olga; Ester → Fira → Irina; Keila → Ekaterina. By her estimate, a noticeable share of renamings (about 15%) obey no logic at all. A match in the table is a hypothesis; only a bridging document ("same person" certificate, a statement, a court decision) confirms it. More on Soviet name changes — [Special groups and name changes](04-special-groups-name-changes.md), section 6.

**A double name for the father — different patronymics for the children.** Sons could receive a patronymic from one half of the double name, daughters from the other, and also from the Russian form: full brothers and sisters end up as "Абрамович", "Александрович", "Гершевна", "Григорьевна" (N. Lipes). Try both halves of the father's double name. Different patronymics among the children do not disprove kinship.

**A name could be changed during life too** — after an illness, business failures, or when a daughter married a man with the same name as her father. Children recorded under the father's old name get the new patronymic in a census. Identity is checked by the family number in the revision, the names of the closest relatives, age and registration (N. Lipes).

**The patronymic in Jewish documents** is written in different ways: "Мошковичъ", "Мошковъ сынъ", "Мошков" are all the same thing.

**On gravestones** the name in Hebrew is written with the father's name: "ben …" (son of so-and-so), "bat …" (daughter of so-and-so). This is often the only source of the father's name.

Name dictionaries: A. Beider, "Handbook of Ashkenazic Given Names and Their Variants" (15,000 variants from 735 root names); B. Feldblyum, "Russian-Jewish Given Names" (more than 6,000 names). See [Dictionaries and encyclopedias](../../reading/dictionaries-and-encyclopedias.md).

## 7. The custom of naming after the dead

Among Ashkenazim a child was named after a **dead** relative, usually a grandfather or grandmother. A living relative's name was not "borrowed". The JewishGen FAQ notes that this gives you a clue to generations.

How to use it:
- the children's names suggest the names of dead grandfathers. An example from one study: a boy Moisei (Moisei), born in 1868, was probably named after a grandfather Moshe who had died by then;
- if a family has two grandsons with the same name from different sons, they most likely share a dead grandfather of that name;
- this is a **hint, not proof**.

Two more consequences of the custom:
- **full namesakes "uncle — nephew"** of similar age are a frequent reason for mixing up generations;
- **the set of names** repeats in a family across generations. A match not of one name but of a set of names over two or three generations (for example, in censuses of different years) is a strong sign of one family (Am HaZikaron, N. Lipes). From a surname derived from a male name (Gershovich, Leibzon) you can see which male name was the family's own; from a surname derived from a female name (Sorkin, Rozenson), which female name [the opinion of the Am HaZikaron institute; check against Beider's dictionaries].

## 8. Double surnames "alias" (*on zhe*)

You find entries like "Нудельманъ онъ же Гольштейнъ" (Nudelman alias Golshtein) or "Хуна Михелевъ Вайнбергъ /онъ же Репетуръ/" (Khuna Mikhelev Vainberg /alias Repetur/). It is a strong sign: such a link is rare and helps tell a family apart from people with the same surname. It also explains why a person "vanishes" under one surname and "appears" under another.

## 9. Emigrants and a change of surname

- **The myth:** "the surname was changed on Ellis Island". The JewishGen FAQ and the US National Park Service say that the inspectors worked from manifests compiled by the steamship companies at the port of departure. Immigrants chose new names themselves, more often after arrival, usually at naturalisation (Moshe → Morris, Mendel → Milton).
- A surname could be changed completely. A real example: the emigrant Moische Shefel became Max Shechter in the USA.
- The new name is found in the naturalisation file (the column "original name if changed").
- If the surname is unrecognisable, search by **place of birth, age, the mother's maiden name, the relative he was going to**.

In detail — [Emigration](13-emigration.md).

## 9a. When a namesake is a relative and when not

- A surname **derived from a place name or a trade** borne by namesakes in different places is by default not kinship: such surnames arose independently. The bearer of a surname derived from a place name more often lived **not** in the place of that name (N. Lipes).
- A rare, unexplained surname in one locality is more likely kin.
- In the Kingdom of Poland (the rules of 1821) and in Galicia, different families in one locality were supposed to be given different surnames, so there "one surname + one place" is a stronger sign than in the Pale of Settlement [from search snippet].
- Relatives sometimes **deliberately changed a letter** to separate themselves from another family in the same town, or merged with the local form of a surname and became "namesakes" of strangers (N. Lipes).
- A **unique clerk's error** repeated in two independent documents links families more reliably than a match of the correct surname.

How to assemble families and tell namesakes apart — [Assembling the whole family](09-whole-family.md).

## 10. Doubles and noise

Every surname has "doubles" that fill the results:
- namesakes and similar surnames (Shefel / Sheffel / Shepel — Шефель / Шеффель / Шепель);
- ordinary words: "шеффель" (*scheffel*) — a measure of grain in 1860s–70s price lists; "шефеле" (*shefele*) — "little sheep" in the Yiddish press;
- famous people with the same surname (the ataman Shepel in newspapers of 1919–1927).

Check initials, address, profession, age. A yearly cut of newspapers for one surname without a place gives hundreds of irrelevant articles — limit the search by place and years.

## 11. Transliteration: tools

- **Steve Morse One-Step** (stevemorse.org): Russian ↔ English transliteration, Daitch–Mokotoff Soundex and Beider–Morse, Hebrew Soundex.
- Library of Congress transliteration tables.
- In databases with phonetic search (JewishGen, USHMM, JRI-Poland) choose Daitch–Mokotoff or Beider–Morse: they were created for East European and Yiddish surnames.

> **How to ask AI**
>
> *For a plain chat, add: "If you can't open a source, say so; mark fonds, villages and dates you name from memory as [from memory, verify]. Answer in English, and give names, places and archive titles in the original script in parentheses." ([why](../1-ai-search/02-prompt-library.md)). The (browser agent) label is for an AI that opens websites itself; the other prompts suit any chat.*
>
> - "Draw up all the spellings of the surname [name]: Cyrillic (including pre-reform), Latin, Polish, Romanian and German renderings, Yiddish and Hebrew; variants with a different root vowel and without the first letter." *Check yourself:* doubtful forms — against the index of Beider's dictionaries.
> - "The father was called [double name]. What patronymics could his sons and daughters have received, including Russian forms in Soviet documents?" *Check yourself:* a match in the table of Russifications is a hypothesis, not proof.
>
> *Bad → good:* "How do you spell the surname [name] in English?" (one spelling, while clerks wrote the surname in different ways) → "Draw up the spellings of the surname [name] for searching JewishGen: Polish, German and English renderings, a different root vowel; give each also in Cyrillic."
