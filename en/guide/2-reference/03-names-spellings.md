# Names, surnames, spellings

What this section covers: why the same person is called differently in different documents, and how to search so as not to miss your own. This is central to Jewish genealogy, but much of it holds for everyone.

---

## 1. Surnames did not appear all at once

For the Jews of the Russian Empire, surnames were fixed by the law of 1804 and finally settled by the 1830s. Earlier there is often no surname at all, and a "surname" in a document may be a nickname, a trade or a place name.

What follows from this:
- before the 1820s search by **given name + patronymic + age + community**, not by surname;
- brothers in one family could register **under different surnames**. A real example: in the 1811 census of the shtetl Dunaivtsi, three full brothers, sons of one father, are recorded under three different nicknames — Abramko Shefel, Aron Gruz, Mendel Krotnik;
- in revision lists the family number is not preserved from one revision to the next — generations have to be linked by ages (see [Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](01-records-empire-ussr.md)).

## 2. One surname — many spellings

The same surname was written down by clerks in Russian, Polish, Romanian and German; people themselves wrote it in Yiddish and Hebrew; in emigration, in English. Add the reading errors of indexers.

Real examples:
- **Shefel** (Шефель) turned up as Shafel, Sheffel, Shefal, Sheifel, Sheffel, Schefel, Scheffel, Schaffel, Szefel, Shafel, Sofiel — about 20 variants in all. In the JewishGen indexes it is hardly anywhere recorded as SHEFEL;
- **Vainberg / Weinberg**: Вейнберг, Wainberg, Weinberg, Vainberg, Vainbarg (in an Estonian catalogue). A printed list of students had *Wainberg*, while text recognition produced "Weinberg"; in the index the "Wai-" and "Wei-" blocks ended up on different pages.

What to do:
1. Make a **list of all variants** before you begin and add to it as you go.
2. In databases begin with the **"exact"** mode, then add **phonetic** search. The difference can be huge: for one surname, 122 exact records against 2,293 phonetic ones.
3. Remember that phonetics lies too: a query for "Kessel" returned Kishel and Kisel, not Kesel.
4. Check the reading against dictionaries: if a surname is not in Beider's dictionaries, it is almost certainly a misreading. The index of Beider's dictionaries is freely available on stevemorse.org (see [Dictionaries, encyclopedias, reference works](../../reading/dictionaries-and-encyclopedias.md)).
5. **Run through the vowel of the root**: Plet — Plit — Plat — Plot. This is how N. Lipes found a family in the voter lists of a crown rabbi under a form that was not in the database for the district (isrageo.com, 2021). B/V/P/F and vowels are interchangeable in old records.
6. Look at a surname's **range** in the earliest lists with surnames — 1805–1816 — not in the voter lists of 1906–1907 on which the geography of Beider's dictionary rests: by the 1900s families had scattered (N. Lipes).

⚠️ Similar surnames are not necessarily variants of one. "Шефтманъ" (Sheftman) next to "Шефелемъ" (Shefel) in the same shtetl is an independent surname of a different root. Check against a dictionary, not by resemblance to the eye.

## 3. Place names are also spelled in different ways

- Transliteration decides: on JewishGen a query for "Kamenets-Podolsk" gave 0, "Kamenets" gave 8.
- In American ship manifests towns are distorted beyond recognition: Pokotilov is written "Pocolifoff", Proskurov as "Proskaw".
- Catalogues give the name in different cases and in different languages: "Білилівка / Белиловка" (Bilylivka / Belilovka).
- In Hebrew and Yiddish a town has several spellings: Izmail — איזמאיל and איזמאהיל.

Check distorted names against the JewishGen Gazetteer and the Communities Database (about 2.4 million places; more than 6,000 communities with name variants and Yiddish synonyms).

## 4. Recognised text (OCR) errs predictably

Text-recognition software on old books:
- **"eats" or replaces the first letter**: Вайнбергъ (Vainberg) turns into "Байнбергъ" and "айнбергь". Search **without the first letter**: "ефель" (-efel), "айнберг" (-ainberg);
- confuses similar letters, and in Hebrew, final and ordinary forms (ב read as כ);
- mixes up columns in directories: neighbouring lines in the text are not necessarily neighbours on the page.

So a zero in a full-text search proves nothing in itself (see "control record" in [Standard of proof](../3-results/02-standard-of-proof.md)).

Russian reference works are recognised in **Cyrillic**. On Genealogy Indexer "Shefel" in Latin letters gave 1 match in the whole database, "Шефель" gave 15, "Шеффель" gave 92. FamilySearch also writes that for Soviet military databases the Russian spelling gives noticeably more than a transliteration does.

Try **both orthographies** — pre-reform and modern. A Google Books search for the phrase "Вайнбергъ Моисей" (with the hard sign at the end) found a directory that had not turned up for two weeks with an ordinary query. Yandex "Search in Archives" (*Poisk po arkhivam*, a Russian-language site) understands pre-reform spelling and grammatical cases by itself.

## 5. Handwriting: which letters get confused

In 19th-century manuscripts the following are often confused: Б and В, Ш and Т, the endings "-ель" and "-еръ", Р and К. How to check:
- compare how **the same clerk** writes these letters in neighbouring entries;
- enlarge the word in question rather than reading the whole page at once;
- do not fit the reading to your expectation. A real case: "Михель Кр…торъ" was wanted as "Репиторъ", but on enlargement it turned out to be "Кройторъ".

## 6. Jewish names: double, everyday, Russified

**Double names** (Berko-Duvid, Khaim-Iosi, Meishe-Leiba) are one name. A clerk might write down only half. In the marriage index of Chișinău (Kishinev) one can see that "Berko-Duvid" is a double name, not "Berko, son of Duvid": for other grooms the patronymics there are written with the ending "-ov".

**One name in different forms.** Useful correspondences:

| In the document | Full Jewish name |
|---|---|
| Берко (Berko) | Dov-Ber |
| Сруль (Srul) | Yisrael |
| Мошко, Мовша (Moshko, Movsha) | Moshe |
| Лейзер, Лейзор (Leizer, Leizor) | Eliezer |

**A Russian form beside the Jewish one** is common in Soviet documents: Gersh Shliumovich = Grigorii Solomonovich; Movsha = Mikhail; Khaya = Klara; Berko = Boris; Srul = Izrail. The Soviet "Rossiiskii meditsinskii spisok" [Russian Medical Directory] of 1924 often gives both forms side by side.

**Russifications are tendencies, not rules.** N. Lipes gives a long list of non-trivial pairs (nadialipes.info, the article "Names"): Leib (Arye-Leib) → Lev **or Aleksei**; Akiva → Kiva → Nikolai; Avigdor → Viktor; Nisan → Andrei; Motl → Matvei or Dmitrii; Efraim, Faivish → Fedor or Filipp; Usher → Yurii or Georgii; Kopel → Yakov; Ber, Berko, Bentsion, Benyamin → Boris; Issakhar → Aleksandr. Women's names: Shneidl → Evgeniya; Zisl, Zeidl → Zoya or Zinaida; Golda ↔ Olga; Ester → Fira → Irina; Keila → Ekaterina. By her estimate, a noticeable share of renamings (about 15%) follows no logic at all. A match in a table is a hypothesis; it is confirmed only by a bridging document ("same person" [*on zhe*] certificate, a court decision). More on Soviet name changes — [Special groups and name changes](04-special-groups-name-changes.md), section 6.

**A father's double name — different patronymics for the children.** Sons could receive a patronymic from one half of the double name, daughters from the other, and also from the Russian form: full siblings come out as "Abramovich", "Aleksandrovich", "Gershevna", "Grigorevna" (N. Lipes). Run through both halves of the father's double name. Different patronymics for the children do not disprove kinship.

**A name was also changed during life** — after an illness, business failures, or when a daughter married a namesake of her father. Children recorded under the father's old name get the new patronymic in a census. Identity is checked by the family number in the revision, the names of close relatives, age and registration (N. Lipes).

**The patronymic in Jewish documents** is written in different ways: "Мошковичъ", "Мошковъ сынъ" [Moshkov's son], "Мошков" — all the same thing.

**On gravestones** the Hebrew gives the name with the father's name: "ben …" (son of so-and-so), "bat …" (daughter of so-and-so). This is often the only source of the father's name.

Name dictionaries: A. Beider, "Handbook of Ashkenazic Given Names and Their Variants" (15,000 variants from 735 root names); B. Feldblyum, "Russian-Jewish Given Names" (more than 6,000 names). See [Dictionaries, encyclopedias, reference works](../../reading/dictionaries-and-encyclopedias.md).

## 7. The custom of naming after the dead

Among Ashkenazim a child was named after a **deceased** relative, usually a grandfather or grandmother. A living relative's name was not "borrowed". The JewishGen FAQ notes that this gives a hint about the generations.

How to use it:
- the children's names suggest the names of deceased grandfathers. An example from one study: a boy Moisei, born in 1868, was probably named after a grandfather Moshe who had died by then;
- if a family has two grandchildren with the same name from different sons, they most likely share a deceased grandfather with that name;
- this is a **hint, not proof**.

Two more consequences of the custom:
- **full namesakes "uncle — nephew"** of similar age are a frequent reason for confusing generations;
- **a set of names** repeats in a lineage across generations. A match not of one name but of a set of names across two or three generations (for example, in censuses of different years) is a strong sign of one family (Am HaZikaron, N. Lipes). From a surname derived from a male name (Gershovich, Leibzon) one sees which male name was the family's own; from a surname derived from a female name (Sorkin, Rozenson), which female name [opinion of the Am HaZikaron Institute, to be checked against Beider's dictionaries].

## 8. Double surnames "on zhe" ("a.k.a.")

Entries occur such as "Нудельманъ онъ же Гольштейнъ" [Nudelman a.k.a. Golshtein] or "Хуна Михелевъ Вайнбергъ /онъ же Репетуръ/" [Khuna Mikhelev Vainberg /a.k.a. Repetur/]. This is a strong sign: such a link is rare and helps to tell a family from people who merely share a surname. It also explains why a person "disappears" under one surname and "appears" under another.

## 9. Emigrants and surname changes

- **Myth:** "the surname was changed on Ellis Island." The JewishGen FAQ and the US National Park Service write that inspectors worked from manifests compiled by the steamship companies at the port of embarkation. Immigrants chose new names themselves, more often after arrival, usually at naturalisation (Moshe → Morris, Mendel → Milton).
- A surname could be changed completely. A real example: the emigrant Moische Shefel became Max Shechter in the USA.
- The new name is found in the naturalisation file (the column "original name if changed").
- If the surname is unrecognisable, search by **place of birth, age, the mother's maiden name, the relative he was going to join**.

In detail — [Emigration](13-emigration.md).

## 9a. When a namesake is a relative and when not

- A surname **from a place name or from a trade**, borne by namesakes in different places, is by default not kinship: such surnames arose independently. The bearer of a surname from a place name more often lived **not** in the place of that name (N. Lipes).
- A rare, unexplained surname in one locality is more likely kin.
- In Congress Poland (the rules of 1821) and in Galicia, different families of one locality were supposed to be given different surnames, so there "one surname + one place" is a stronger sign than in the Pale of Settlement [from search snippet].
- Relatives sometimes **deliberately changed a letter** in order to separate from another family in the same town, or merged with the local form of the surname and became "namesakes" of strangers (N. Lipes).
- A **unique clerk's error** repeated in two independent documents links families more reliably than a match of the correct surname.

How to gather families and sort out namesakes — [Assemble the whole family](09-whole-family.md).

## 10. Doubles and noise

Every surname has "doubles" that fill the results:
- namesakes and similar surnames (Shefel / Sheffel / Shepel);
- ordinary words: "sheffel" (шеффель) was a grain measure in 1860s–70s price lists; "shefele" is "little sheep" in the Yiddish press;
- famous people with the same surname (the ataman Shepel in newspapers of 1919–1927).

Check initials, address, profession, age. A year-by-year slice of newspapers for one surname without a place gives hundreds of irrelevant articles — limit the search by place and years.

## 11. Transliteration: tools

- **Steve Morse One-Step** (stevemorse.org): Russian ↔ English transliteration, Daitch-Mokotoff Soundex and Beider-Morse, Hebrew Soundex.
- Library of Congress transliteration tables.
- In databases with phonetic search (JewishGen, USHMM, JRI-Poland) choose Daitch-Mokotoff or Beider-Morse: they were created for Eastern European and Yiddish surnames.

> **How to ask AI**
>
> - "Draw up all the spellings of the surname [N]: Cyrillic (including pre-reform), Latin, Polish, Romanian and German renderings, Yiddish and Hebrew; variants with a different root vowel and without the first letter." *Check yourself:* doubtful forms — against the index of Beider's dictionaries.
> - "The father was called [double name]. What patronymics could his sons and daughters have received, including Russian forms in Soviet documents? The documents may be in Russian." *Check yourself:* a match in a table of Russifications is a hypothesis, not proof.
> - "Here are the names of the children in a family: [list with years]. After which deceased relatives might they have been named? What does this say about the generation above?" *Check yourself:* this is a hint, not proof; there are exceptions.
> - "Here are two forms of a surname from different documents: [A] and [B]. Are they variants of one surname, a clerk's error, or different surnames? If the forms are in Cyrillic, please consider Russian pre-1918 spelling." *Check yourself:* resemblance to the eye is not an argument; check against a dictionary and other signs.
