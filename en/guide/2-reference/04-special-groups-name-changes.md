# Special groups and name changes

What this section covers: where to look for the documents of converts, cantonists, Krymchaks and Karaites, Bukharan and Mountain Jews, and Subbotniks. And how names changed in Soviet times: Khaim becomes Efim, Moishe becomes Mikhail, and the children's patronymics may be "reworked" even if the father did not change his name.

Markers: **[checked]** — the page was read; **[from search snippet]** — information from search results, the page was not opened; check it yourself before quoting. For several groups only starting points are given here, not ready-made methods; this is stated openly.

## 1. Converts to Christianity (*vykresty*)

**Where the conversion is noted** (arch.expert, "Vykresty: kto oni i kak naiti dokumenty" [Converts: who they are and how to find documents]) [page read; commercial site, author not named] (Russian-language site):
- in **Orthodox metrical books** — notes such as "iz evreev" [of the Jews], "kreshchenyi iudei" [baptised Jew];
- in the **files of the spiritual consistory** on conversion to Orthodoxy: petitions, permissions;
- in **revision lists** before 1858 — "prinyal pravoslavie" [adopted Orthodoxy];
- in the **documents of educational institutions**.

**Order of search** (same source):
1. place;
2. the children's metrical records;
3. the baptism record;
4. the consistory file;
5. address books.

After baptism a person often has a new name and patronymic (after the godfather). So search first by place and years, not by name.

- Notices of baptisms were printed in the **diocesan gazettes** (*eparkhialnye vedomosti*; for example, "Kievskie eparkhialnye vedomosti") [from search snippet].
- The St Petersburg fonds (consistory, metrical books, gymnasiums) are discussed in an article by A. Khaesh in the journal "Zametki po evreiskoi istorii" (berkovich-zametki.com, 2015, "Starina", no. 1) [checked] (Russian-language site). The fond reference codes from that article are not given here: in the summary they were not checked against the article itself. Take them from the article.

## 2. Cantonists

Jewish boys were taken as recruits in **1827–1856**. In family documents a boy may "disappear" in this period.

- **Metrical books, confession registers and marriage inquiries of cantonists** are held in **RGVIA** (Russian State Military Historical Archive) [from search snippet: VGD forum].
- There are files on the distribution of underage Jewish recruits. An example from the VGD forum: **RGVIA, f. 405, op. 2, d. 4342** (the 96th levy, 1831–1832) [from search snippet — reference code not checked against the inventory].
- The topic "Cantonists" on the j-roots forum has more than 27 pages of discussion (forum.j-roots.info, viewtopic.php?t=66) [from search snippet] (Russian-language site).

Many cantonists were baptised — then they are searched for by the rules for converts as well (section 1).

A Russian-sounding surname in a Jew may derive from cantonists: according to N. Lipes, their patronymic sometimes became a surname (nadialipes.info, the article on Jewish surnames).

## 3. Krymchaks and Karaites

- **The census of the Krymchak community of 1913** is the main source: held in the Museum of Ethnography in St Petersburg, about 5,500 people, 19 settlements, with family traditions of origin (Avotaynu Online, "The Jews of the Crimea and their Names", 2015) [checked].
- The same source collects **114 Krymchak surnames** with an analysis of origin: Hebrew, Turkic, Romance, Slavic languages, Yiddish [checked].
- The leading researcher on the subject is **Mikhail Kizilov** (more than 200 articles) [from search snippet].
- For Karaites the main source is the **gravestones of Karaite cemeteries** [from search snippet].

## 4. Bukharan and Mountain Jews

- **Bukharan Jews**: family lists of 1870–1910, land contracts of the 19th century, community records, memoirs — Albert Kaganovitch, "Genealogy of Bukharan Jewry: Genealogical Sources and Issues" (Avotaynu, 2012; available on academia.edu) [from search snippet].
- **Mountain Jews**: no genealogical guide could be found. If you know a good guide, it is worth adding here.

## 5. Subbotniks and *gerim* (converts to Judaism)

Entries "iz sektantov-subbotnikov" [of the sectarian Subbotniks] occur in the **metrical books of the Irkutsk synagogue** and in the **metrical books of Orthodox churches** [from search snippet: an article on CyberLeninka about the Subbotniks of Irkutsk and Yenisei guberniyas; topics on the VGD forum (no. 82565) and j-roots (t=5107)].

Subbotniks are Russians by origin who adopted Jewish rites. So they are looked for in both synagogue and Orthodox metrical books.

Descendants of Subbotniks and Molokans who converted to Judaism could keep Russian surnames (for example, "Matveev") — this is one more explanation for a "non-Jewish" surname in a Jewish family (N. Lipes).

## 6. ⭐ Soviet name changes

### What the study showed

Svetlana Amosova and Svetlana Nikolaeva, "Praktiki peremeny imen u evreev Podolii i Bukoviny v sovetskii period" [Practices of name change among the Jews of Podolia and Bukovina in the Soviet period] (collection "Kultura slavyan i kultura evreev: dialog, skhodstva, razlichiya", 2010; available on cyberleninka.ru) [checked] (in Russian). The material is field interviews of 2005–2009 in Tulchyn, Mohyliv-Podilskyi, Balta and Chernivtsi, and ZAGS (Soviet civil registry) records of 1951–1975.

- **Three names for one person**: in documents, at home and in the synagogue. The official "neutral" Russian name might match neither the home name nor the synagogue name.
- **The transition often goes through a diminutive form**: traditional name → neutral diminutive → full modern name. An example from the article: **Moishe → Misha → Mikhail**.
- **Stable pairs** from the article's tables:

| Traditional name | Soviet name |
|---|---|
| Moishe | Misha → Mikhail |
| Khaim | Efim |
| Borukh | Boris |
| Abram | Arkadii |
| Srul | Sasha, Aleksandr |
| Isaak | Aleksandr |
| Rakhil | Raisa |
| Khana | Anna |

### ⚠️ The patronymic was changed too

It is sometimes written that the father's original name "is preserved in the patronymic". The article by Amosova and Nikolaeva describes the **opposite** as well: the name was often changed **only in the children's patronymic**, although the father himself changed it neither in documents nor in daily life. An example from the article: "Borisovna instead of Berkovna". The authors write that such a replacement in the patronymic was apparently very widespread. It could also be made by registrars — by mistake or of their own will.

What this means for the search:
- the father in the documents is **Berko**, the daughter is **Borisovna**. This may be one family;
- run through the patronymic using the table of pairs: "Efimovich" may stand with the son of a Khaim who himself did not change his name;
- the same person may have different names in different documents: check by a **second identifier** (year and place of birth, names of relatives, address).

More name correspondences (Berko → Boris, Movsha → Mikhail, Khaya → Klara, Gersh Shliumovich → Grigorii Solomonovich) — in [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md), section 6.

### Discrepancies in records of 1928–1933

According to N. Lipes, from about 1928 to 1933 ZAGS did not ask parents for documents when registering a child, and a mother could be recorded under a new, "Russian" name. Later the discrepancy was legalised — by "same person" (*on zhe*) certificates or in court (for example, for the sake of an inheritance). Look for such certificates and rulings establishing a fact: this is the bridging document between two names (isrageo.com, 2019).

A table of frequent Russifications — [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md), section 6.

### Official change of name and surname

- Under the **decree of 1924**, a change of surname or name was announced by a **notice in the local official newspaper**. An example is "Vestnik Leningradskogo oblispolkoma" [Bulletin of the Leningrad Oblast Executive Committee] of 1924–1932 [from search snippet]. Look for such notices in the newspapers of the city where the person lived at the time of the change. How to search newspapers — [Press](../../catalog/press.md).
- For the rules on recording nationality in the passport (the regulation of 1974) there is information only from search results (Wikipedia) — unverified.
- For citizenship documents, a name change is confirmed by a document on the change of name or by an archival certificate of identity ([Citizenship by descent](14-citizenship-by-descent.md), section 10).

> **How to ask AI**
>
> - "In Soviet documents the person is [Russian full name], while in pre-revolutionary ones he should appear under a Jewish name. Which Jewish names and patronymics could correspond to this Russian form? Please take into account Soviet-era Russian naming practices." *Check yourself:* there are no rules, only tendencies; you need a bridging document (*on zhe*, a certificate, a court ruling).
> - "The surname [N] does not sound Jewish, but the family is Jewish. What explanations are possible (cantonists, converts, Subbotniks, a change of surname) and which documents would test them? Relevant sources may be in Russian." *Check yourself:* a theory of baptism or cantonist ancestry needs a document, not a guess.
