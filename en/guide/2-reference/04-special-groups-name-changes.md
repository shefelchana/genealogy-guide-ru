# Special groups and name changes

What this section covers: where to look for the documents of converts, cantonists, Krymchaks and Karaites, Bukharan and Mountain Jews, Subbotniks. And how names changed in Soviet times: Khaim becomes Yefim, Moishe becomes Mikhail, and the children's patronymic can be "redone" even if the father did not change his name.

The markers in square brackets ([checked], [from search snippet], [unverified] and others) are explained in the [Glossary](../0-start/06-glossary.md). For several groups there are only starting points here, not ready-made methods — and this is said openly.

---

## 1. Converts (those who went over to Christianity)

**Where the conversion is noted** (arch.expert, "Vykresty: kto oni i kak nayti dokumenty" [Converts: who they are and how to find the documents]) [checked; a commercial site, author not named]:
- in **Orthodox metrical books** — notes such as "from the Jews" (*iz evreev*), "baptised Jew" (*kreshchenyi iudei*);
- in the files of the **spiritual consistory** on conversion to Orthodoxy: petitions, permissions;
- in **revision lists** before 1858 — "accepted Orthodoxy" (*prinyal pravoslavie*);
- in the **documents of educational institutions**.

**Order of search** (same source):
1. the place;
2. the children's metrical records;
3. the baptism record;
4. the consistory file;
5. address books.

After baptism a person often has a new name and patronymic (after the godfather). So search first by place and years, not by name.

- Reports of baptisms were printed in **diocesan gazettes** (for example, "Kievskie eparkhialnye vedomosti", Kyiv Diocesan Gazette) [from search snippet].
- The St Petersburg fonds (consistory, metrical books, gymnasiums) are analysed in an article by A. Khaesh in the journal "Zametki po evreiskoi istorii" (berkovich-zametki.com, 2015, "Starina", No. 1) [checked]. The fond numbers from this article are not given here: in the retelling they were not checked against the article itself. Take them from the article.

## 2. Cantonists

Jewish boys were taken as recruits in **1827–1856**. In family documents a boy can "vanish" in this period.

- **Metrical records, confessional rolls and marriage investigations of cantonists** are held in the **Russian State Military Historical Archive** (РГВИА, RGVIA) [from search snippet: the VGD forum, Всероссийское генеалогическое древо].
- There are files on the distribution of underage Jewish recruits. An example from the VGD forum: **RGVIA, fond 405, inventory 2, file 4342** (the 96th levy, 1831–1832) [from search snippet — the reference was not checked against the inventory].
- The "Cantonists" topic on the j-roots forum (the Jewish Roots forum, Еврейские корни) — more than 27 pages of discussion (forum.j-roots.info, viewtopic.php?t=66) [from search snippet].

Many cantonists were baptised — then they are also searched by the rules for converts (section 1).

A Russian-sounding surname in a Jew may come from cantonists: according to N. Lipes, their patronymic sometimes became the surname (nadialipes.info, an article on Jewish surnames).

## 3. Krymchaks and Karaites

- **The census of the Krymchak community of 1913** is the main source: held in the Museum of Ethnography in St Petersburg, about 5,500 people, 19 settlements, with family traditions of origin (Avotaynu Online, "The Jews of the Crimea and their Names", 2015) [checked].
- The same source collects **114 Krymchak surnames** with an analysis of their origin: Hebrew, Turkic, Romance and Slavic languages, Yiddish [checked].
- The leading researcher of the subject is **Mikhail Kizilov** (more than 200 articles) [from search snippet].
- For Karaites the main source is the **gravestones of Karaite cemeteries** [from search snippet].

## 4. Bukharan and Mountain Jews

- **Bukharan Jews**: family lists of 1870–1910, land contracts of the 19th century, community records, memoirs — Albert Kaganovitch, "Genealogy of Bukharan Jewry: Genealogical Sources and Issues" (Avotaynu, 2012; on academia.edu) [from search snippet].
- **Mountain Jews**: the compiler did not come across a genealogical guide. If you know a good one, it deserves to be added here.

## 5. Subbotniks and gerim

Entries "from the sectarian Subbotniks" turn up in the **metrical books of the Irkutsk synagogue** and in the **metrical books of Orthodox churches** [from search snippet: an article on CyberLeninka about the Subbotniks of Irkutsk and Yenisei guberniyas; topics on the VGD forum (No. 82565) and j-roots (t=5107)].

Subbotniks are Russians by origin who adopted Jewish rites. So they are searched in both synagogue and Orthodox metrical books.

Descendants of Subbotniks and of Molokans who converted to Judaism could keep Russian surnames (for example "Matveev", Матвеевы) — this is one more explanation for a "non-Jewish" surname in a Jewish family (N. Lipes).

## 6. ⭐ Soviet name changes

### What the research showed

Svetlana Amosova and Svetlana Nikolaeva, "Praktiki peremeny imen u evreev Podolii i Bukoviny v sovetskii period" (Practices of name change among the Jews of Podolia and Bukovina in the Soviet period; in the collection "Kultura slavyan i kultura evreev: dialog, skhodstva, razlichiya", 2010; on cyberleninka.ru — in Russian) [checked]. The material: field interviews of 2005–2009 in Tulchyn, Mohyliv-Podilskyi, Balta and Chernivtsi, and ZAGS records of 1951–1975.

- **Three names for one person**: in documents, at home and in the synagogue. The official "neutral" Russian name might match neither the home name nor the synagogue name.
- **The change often goes through a diminutive**: traditional name → neutral diminutive → full modern name. An example from the article: **Moishe → Misha → Mikhail** (Мойше → Миша → Михаил).
- **Stable pairs** from the article's tables:

| Traditional name | Soviet name |
|---|---|
| Moishe (Мойше) | Misha → Mikhail |
| Khaim (Хаим) | Yefim |
| Borukh (Борух) | Boris |
| Abram (Абрам) | Arkady |
| Srul (Сруль) | Sasha, Aleksandr |
| Isaak (Исаак) | Aleksandr |
| Rakhil (Рахиль) | Raisa |
| Khana (Хана) | Anna |

### ⚠️ The patronymic was changed too

Sometimes it is written that the father's original name "is preserved in the patronymic". The article by Amosova and Nikolaeva also describes the **opposite**: the name was often changed **only in the children's patronymic**, although the father himself changed it neither in documents nor in daily life. An example from the article: "Borisovna instead of Berkovna" (Борисовна вместо Берковна). The authors write that such a replacement in the patronymic was apparently very common. It could also have been made by registrars — by mistake or at their own will.

What this means for your search:
- the father is **Berko** in documents, the daughter is **Borisovna**. It may be one family;
- try the patronymic through the table of pairs: "Yefimovich" (Ефимович) may be borne by the son of a Khaim who never changed his own name;
- the same person in different documents can have different names: check by a **second feature** (year and place of birth, names of relatives, address).

More name correspondences (Berko → Boris, Movsha → Mikhail, Khaya → Klara, Gersh Shlyumovich → Grigory Solomonovich) are in [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md), section 6.

### Discrepancies in records of 1928–1933

According to N. Lipes, from about 1928 to 1933 ZAGS did not ask the parents for documents when registering a child, and the mother could be recorded under a new, "Russian" name. Later the discrepancy was legalised — by "same person" (*on zhe*) certificates or in court (for example, for an inheritance). Look for such certificates and decisions establishing a fact: they are the bridging document between the two names (isrageo.com, 2019).

A table of frequent Russifications — [Names, surnames, spellings](03-names-spellings.md), section 6.

### Official change of name and surname

- Under the **decree of 1924** a person changing a surname or name had to place a **notice in the local official newspaper**. An example is "Vestnik Leningradskogo oblispolkoma" (Herald of the Leningrad Oblast Executive Committee) of 1924–1932 [from search snippet]. Look for such notices in the newspapers of the city where the person lived at the time of the change. How to search newspapers — [Press](../../catalog/press.md).
- On the rules for recording nationality in the passport (the regulation of 1974) there is only information from search results (Wikipedia) — unverified.
- For citizenship documents a name change is confirmed by a document on the change of name or an archival certificate of identity ([Citizenship by descent](14-citizenship-by-descent.md), section 10).

> **How to ask AI**
>
> *For a plain chat, add: "If you can't open a source, say so; mark fonds, villages and dates you name from memory as [from memory, verify]. Answer in English, and give names, places and archive titles in the original script in parentheses." ([why](../1-ai-search/02-prompt-library.md)). The (browser agent) label is for an AI that opens websites itself; the other prompts suit any chat.*
>
> - "In Soviet documents the person is [Russian full name], and in pre-revolutionary ones he should be under a Jewish name. Which Jewish names and patronymics could correspond to this Russian form?" *Check yourself:* there are no rules, only tendencies; you need a bridging document ("same person" certificate, a statement, a court decision).
> - "The surname [name] does not sound Jewish, but the family is Jewish. What explanations are possible (cantonists, converts, Subbotniks, a change of surname) and which documents would test them?" *Check yourself:* a version about baptism or cantonists needs a document, not a guess.
>
> *Bad → good:* "What was Grigory's Jewish name?" (there is no single answer: no rules, only tendencies) → "In Soviet documents [full name, year of birth, place]: which Jewish names could stand behind this form, and which document will connect them?"
