# Checking results: what to do with an AI answer

What this section covers: how to check a find brought by an AI assistant or agent before recording it in your files, let alone in a tree. A short working order; the theory of proof is in Part 3, [Standard of proof](../3-results/02-standard-of-proof.md).

The main rule, repeated by everyone who works with AI in genealogy (including participants of the IAJGS AI Virtual Summit, 2026): **an AI answer is a hypothesis, not a source**; it must be checked, checked and checked again.

---

## 1. An answer without an address is not a find

Every find must have an **address** (archive, fond-inventory-file-folio, or site, collection, film, frame, record number) and a **verbatim quotation** in the original spelling. If they are missing, ask for them. If the AI cannot give them, it is not a find but a retelling.

An example from a Jewish genealogy group (2026): a person asked a chatbot about an ancestor, it replied that "a burial is recorded", and the person came to the group for confirmation. There was no record that could be cited.

## 2. Order of checking

1. **Open the indicated frame yourself.** Not from a retelling, but the scan itself.
2. **Check the quotation letter for letter**, enlarging the fragment. Especially names, dates, ages, numbers: here AI errs most often. On manuscripts the models read the printed form well and handwritten names and places worse.
3. **Check the fields.** Whether the groom's father and the bride's father are mixed up, a note of estate and a surname, the age "as of the last revision" and the current one.
4. **Check the control record**: if you gave a record known to exist, did the AI find it and read it correctly.
5. **Look for a second independent feature**: patronymic, age, place, names of relatives, occupation. The index and the scan of the same record are one piece of evidence.
6. **Record at once** — in the finds log, with the note "checked against the scan" and the date.

## 3. How to read with AI so that there are fewer errors

- **Two passes.** In the first, give the document type, year and place but **not the expected names**: AI tends to see what it is looking for. In the second — comparison with your hypotheses.
- **High resolution and a fragment.** Louis Kessler (2024), at low resolution, got nothing usable; what helped was a fragment in high resolution plus context (who, to whom, when). The father's name, however, was misread by all three models (beholdgenealogy.com).
- **Mark the illegible.** Ask it to write "(?)" or "illegible" in place of anything unreadable and to keep the original spelling rather than guess.

## 4. "Not found" from AI

An empty AI answer means nothing until the following are stated:
- the source and the limits of what was viewed (which frames, years, sections);
- the list of spellings;
- the method of search (index, full text, page by page; exact or phonetic);
- whether a **control record** was found by the same method;
- whether there were site errors (captcha, 403/429, drop-outs). A zero while the site was refusing is a technical zero, not a result.

And the question: was the person obliged to appear in this source?

## 5. Confidence levels

For recording in files, three levels are convenient:

| Level | What is needed |
|---|---|
| 🟢 **Confirmed** | a record directly naming the fact or kinship, read on the scan, plus a second independent feature |
| 🟡 **Strong version** | two or more independent indirect features, no contradictions; the reasoning is written down |
| ⚪ **Hypothesis** | one feature (surname + place, a coincidence of name) |

Into an online tree — only 🟢. The decision about kinship is made by the human, not by AI.

## 6. What AI does not do and should not do

- Does not pass captchas and "I am not a robot" checks.
- Does not enter passwords, create accounts or pay.
- Does not send letters and requests on your behalf without your direct consent.
- Does not replace an archive: if a document is not digitised, AI will not find it.
