# Scenarios: what to do if…

A set of typical situations and the order of action. Each scenario: **when** — **steps** — **how to finish**.
A general rule for all: first write down what is already known and where you have searched (otherwise you will go in circles), then one question, then the search.

*Scenario lettering in this English edition: A. Start, B. Dead ends, C. Verification and results, D. Special tasks (in the Russian original the letters are А, Б, В, Г).*

---

## A. Start

### A1. "I know only the names of my grandparents, nothing more"
1. Gather what is at home: photographs (captions on the back!), documents, work record books, military ID cards, birth/marriage/death certificates, letters, postcards with addresses.
2. Record family memory **verbatim** with the note "so says …". Especially: cities, occupations, wars, evacuation, "relatives abroad".
3. Find the **place**: where the grandparents were born (from passports, certificates, questionnaires). For Jewish families — the shtetl and uezd.
4. Check the Soviet layer (the most accessible): Pamyat Naroda/OBD Memorial (Russian-language), Yad Vashem, evacuation cards, memorial books, the "Open List" (Russian-language), ZAGS scans (Ukraine on Commons), cemeteries.
5. From the oldest person with a known place, step back: metrical books, censuses, revisions of that place.
**Outcome:** a "person — dates — place — source" table for each person you know. More — [Where to start](../0-start/02-where-to-start.md). How to interview relatives — [Talking to relatives](../0-start/03-talking-to-relatives.md); how to date a photograph without a caption — [Old photographs](../2-reference/17-photographs.md).

### A2. "I have only a surname and an approximate region"
1. Check the surname against dictionaries (Beider — for Jewish ones; regional dictionaries) — where it occurs.
2. Search the indexes: JewishGen (all databases, phonetically), FamilySearch, Geni/MyHeritage — **by region**, not across the whole country.
3. Write out all spelling variants (Cyrillic, Latin, Yiddish/Hebrew, Polish, Romanian), including distortions.
4. Look for **clusters**: where the surname occurs many times is, most likely, the ancestral nest.
**Outcome:** 1–3 candidates for the "place of the family" to check against documents. More — [Names, surnames, spellings](../2-reference/03-names-spellings.md), [Indexed databases](../../catalog/indexed-databases.md).

### A3. "I am starting from the American (emigrant) branch"
See [Emigration](../2-reference/13-emigration.md). In brief: death certificate → census → naturalization → ship manifest → name of the shtetl → documents of the shtetl. Check the names of fellow travellers and the destination address: they often lead to relatives.

## B. Dead ends

### B1. "A person vanishes — absent from documents where they should be"
Check in turn:
1. **Spelling**: all variants, without the first letter (recognition eats it), with a different vocalization; letters confused in handwriting (Б/В [B/V], Ш/Т [Sh/T], «-ель/-еръ» [-el/-er]).
2. **Name**: a double name recorded as one half; a Russian form instead of the Jewish one (Berko → Boris, Srul → Israel); a second name. In Soviet documents — a new name (Khaim → Yefim, Moishe → Mikhail) and a "reworked" patronymic of the children: see [Special groups and name changes](../2-reference/04-special-groups-name-changes.md), section 6. If the person converted or was a cantonist — same place, sections 1–2.
3. **Estate and registration**: look where the person was **listed**, not where they lived; meshchane and merchants are in different lists; guild artisans separately.
4. **Border and era**: at that moment the territory could have belonged to another state (southern Bessarabia 1856–1878 — Romania). Check which uezd and guberniya the place belonged to in the right year, and whether there is a second place with the same name: [Places and maps](../2-reference/06-places-maps.md).
5. **Source**: perhaps the needed book did not survive or was not filmed — record that as a result, not as "not found".
6. **Absence within a document** means something only if the person was obliged to be there (for example, a son who transferred to another society is not listed in his father's household — and that is normal). See [Standard of proof](../3-results/02-standard-of-proof.md), the section on "Not found".

### B2. "Many namesakes turned up — which one is mine?"
1. Make a table of candidates: name, patronymic, age, place, occupation, relatives. For each — a timeline: "the impossible" at once cuts off the extras ([Plan, timeline, place guide](../0-start/04-plan-timeline-place.md), section 3).
2. Cut off by **incompatible** features (age, patronymic, place).
3. Look for a **second independent feature** in those who remain: children's names, witnesses, address, profession.
4. Apply the naming custom: a child was named after a **deceased** relative — the children's names hint at the generation above.
5. Do not choose "the most similar" — if two are equal, keep both as hypotheses.
6. If there are many namesakes in one place, assemble each family in full and look for links between families: [Reconstructing the whole family](../2-reference/09-whole-family.md).

### B3. "The document is unreadable"
1. Take the **original scan**, not a preview; enlarge the needed column. Where to learn to read pre-reform cursive, Polish and Latin metrical books, Kurrent — [Handwriting, transliteration, dates](../2-reference/05-handwriting-transliteration-dates.md).
2. Find the **table header** (what each column means; age "as of the last revision" ≠ the current age).
3. Compare the same hand in neighbouring records (how the clerk writes Р, К, Ш, Т [R, K, Sh, T]).
4. Check the reading of the surname against a dictionary: not in the dictionaries — almost certainly an error.
5. Look for a **second copy** of the document (metrical books and revisions existed in several copies) or a second filming.
6. Ask the community (forums, groups, ViewMate) or AI as a "second opinion" — and still check. How to phrase a request so that people answer — [Other people's trees, communities, researchers](../2-reference/18-communities-researchers.md), section 2. See [Groups and communities](../../reading/groups-and-communities.md), the `reading-archive-scans` skill in [../../../skills/](../../../skills/).

### B3a. "I don't know in which file to look for the surname"
1. First the index, then page-turning: community surname lists, JewishGen databases, full-text corpora — [Community surname indexes](../2-reference/08-surname-indexes.md).
2. By the archival reference, check whether the file is online and where (for Ukraine — "Duck Inspector", *Kachynyi Inspector*).
3. If the file has already been read for another surname — finish the unread sections: this is the cheapest find.

### B4. "The index says one thing, the document another"
Believe the document. The index is a hint about where to look. Record the discrepancy (it will help others) and cite the scan.

### B5. "All online sources have been exhausted"
1. **Collateral lines**: brothers, sisters, brothers-in-law, witnesses at weddings, godparents/mohels — their documents may contain yours.
2. **The "circle of acquaintances" (FAN — friends, associates, neighbors)**: neighbours in the house, partners, guarantors — people moved in groups.
3. **Another type of source**: newspapers (announcements, donor lists), directories (merchants, doctors, lawyers), educational institutions, courts, notarial records, the foreigners' police.
4. **Emigrant relatives**: American/European documents often name the shtetl and parents.
5. **DNA**: a test + working with matches (see [DNA](../2-reference/15-dna.md)).
6. **An archive request**: targeted, with an archival reference (see [Requests and letters](../2-reference/20-requests-letters.md)). If you need a paid researcher on the spot — how to choose and what to demand in the report: [Other people's trees, communities, researchers](../2-reference/18-communities-researchers.md), section 3.
7. **Set aside** and record the open questions — in half a year new digitisations will appear.

### B6. "The site won't let me in (captcha, block, geo-restriction)"
1. Stop — do not repeat requests one after another.
2. Pass the captcha yourself; work more slowly (one frame every 5–10 seconds).
3. Geo-restrictions (Russian government sites from abroad and vice versa): use ordinary access from a suitable country or ask acquaintances; do not bypass protection.
4. Check whether the same material is somewhere else (FamilySearch ↔ the archive's site ↔ Commons). More — [Access, captchas, pace](../2-reference/19-access-captchas-pace.md).

## C. Verification and results

### C1. "I think I found it! What next?"
1. Scan and verbatim quotation → a find record ([template](../../templates/finding.md)).
2. Check: two independent features? Contradictions resolved?
3. Update the hypotheses (confirmed/weakened), persons, research log.
4. Only then — into the tree. And a paragraph for the family history.

### C2. "An agent/AI brought a find"
1. Open the indicated frame yourself.
2. Check the quotation letter for letter.
3. Be especially careful with "expected" names from the task: AI tends to see what it is looking for.
4. No address and quotation — no find. The order of checking — [Checking results](04-checking-results.md); how to accept agents' reports — [Working with agents](03-working-with-agents.md), section 5.

## D. Special tasks

### D1. Documents needed for citizenship / repatriation
See [Citizenship by descent](../2-reference/14-citizenship-by-descent.md): which proofs for each generation, where to get them, how to reconcile different spellings of names.

### D2. Reconstruct the fate of someone killed in the war / repressed / who died in the siege
See [War, repression, the siege](../2-reference/12-war-repression-siege.md): databases by region, typical fields of records, how to search with distorted surnames.

### D2a. The family lived in a shtetl in 1918–1922 (pogroms, flight, orphans)
Pogrom victim databases, recipients of aid from landsmanshaftn, orphanage lists, death books of the neighbouring large town — [Pogroms 1918–1922](../2-reference/11-pogroms-1918-1922.md).

### D2b. "Where is my great-grandfather buried?" / read a gravestone
First — whether the cemetery survives, then burial databases, then reading the stone and converting the date — [Cemeteries](../2-reference/10-cemeteries.md).

### D3. Find living relatives (distant ones)
DNA tests, other users' trees (only as a hint, see [Other people's trees, communities, researchers](../2-reference/18-communities-researchers.md)), emigrant branches, letters to namesakes — politely and briefly (see [Requests and letters](../2-reference/20-requests-letters.md)). If DNA showed something unexpected — [Ethics and preservation](../3-results/04-ethics-preservation.md).
