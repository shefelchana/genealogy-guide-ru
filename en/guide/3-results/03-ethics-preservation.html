Ethics and preservation of results

What this section covers: how to handle unexpected DNA finds, difficult discoveries and information about living people; what may be published; how to preserve an archive for decades and to whom to pass documents.

The markers in square brackets ([checked], [from search snippet], [unverified] and others) are explained in the Glossary. ⚠️ Everything about law in this section is a retelling of American authors, not legal advice.

The basic rules of DNA ethics are in DNA, section 7. The 3-2-1 backup rule is in Keeping files, section 5. Here are additions.

1. DNA surprises

  • Consent at every step, not once: when you persuade a relative to take a test, when you manage someone else's test, when you publish results — Judy G. Russell in an interview with Amy Johnson Crow, "Ethics and Genetic Genealogy" [checked].
  • Think of the living, who are not ready for surprises (same place).
  • A match's silence may mean "the person is in shock", not "ignoring" (same place). Do not write again with pressure.
  • The landmark is the standards of GeneticGenealogyStandards.com (same place) [checked].
  • There is a right to know, but no right to a relationship. Do not pressure those who have declined contact. Unexpected parentage (in English NPE — "non-parental event") keep confidential and do not disclose without consent — FamilySearch Wiki, "Ethics and DNA Testing" [checked].
  • Genetic data cannot be replaced: protect your account with a strong password (same place).

If the surprise is yours

Practical advice, not from the sources above:

  • Do not forward the find to relatives at once. First make sure it is not an error (two independent lines of matches, not one).
  • Decide to whom and why it needs to be known. An elderly relative has the right not to find out.

2. Living people and publication

  • Do not publish information about living people without their consent. Hide the names of living DNA matches in publications — per the NGS "Guidelines for Sharing Information with Others" and Judy Russell's article "The Rights of the Living" [from search snippet].
  • Remember that exposures hurt relatives — for example, information about a criminal record (same place) [from search snippet]. The same applies to illegitimate children, illnesses, denunciations.
  • What of this is already in the guide: into an online tree — only what is confirmed and without the living (Keeping files, section 6); sensitive matters in the text of a family history — carefully (Writing the family history, section 2).

In practice:

  • in open trees leave the living hidden (FamilySearch Family Tree does this automatically, see Tree platforms);
  • in a story for a wide circle replace the names of the living with "cousin N.";
  • before publication show the text to those it is about.

3. Difficult finds

Documents about pogroms, shootings, denunciations, deaths of children, renunciation of family can hit hard.

Advice from Trista ("Trista the Genealogist", "Pain in Your Family History") [checked]:

  • give yourself time to live through the find;
  • take breaks;
  • if needed, ask for support from close ones or a specialist;
  • evaluate ancestors in the context of their era.

No separate method for descendants of Holocaust victims from genealogists could be found; there are only psychological studies about the third generation [from search snippet].

How to talk about the war with elderly relatives — Talking to relatives, section 3.

4. Copyright

All the points below are American law as retold by Judy Russell, The Legal Genealogist. Her site did not open on the date of checking, so everything here is [from search snippet].

  • Facts (names, dates, kinship) are not protected by copyright. Notes and reasoning in someone else's tree are protected: permission and attribution of the author are needed ("Using the Work of Others") [from search snippet].
  • The right to a photograph belongs to the photographer, not to the owner of the print. For unpublished pictures the term is the author's life plus 70 years. Amateur pictures are also protected; the trouble is that it is often unknown who took them ("Copyright and the Old Family Photo") [from search snippet].

⚠️ For pictures from Russian and Ukrainian studios of the 19th century the term of protection has apparently long expired, but legally this has not been checked. For Soviet amateur photographs and for publication in a commercial book — verify under the law of your country.

In practice: state where a picture comes from and whose photograph it is; for someone else's photo (for example, a gravestone on Find a Grave) ask permission from the person who took it (Requests and letters, section "Volunteers").

5. How to store an archive: the Library of Congress order

The Library of Congress, "Personal Archiving: Photos" [checked]:

  1. Find all files — on all disks, phones, in clouds.
  2. Select what is worth keeping.
  3. Name clearly and add a description (who is in the photo, where the document comes from). How to name scans — Keeping files, section 4.
  4. At least two copies — in places physically distant from each other.
  5. Check once a year that the copies open.
  6. Move to a new medium roughly every 5 years.
  7. Keep the inventory with the important papers.

Scans: a master copy in TIFF and a working one in JPEG (same place).

6. Three copies and an heir

FamilySearch Newsroom ("How to Manage Your Family's Digital Assets", the rule as formulated by Dick Eastman) [checked]:

  • three copies, two types of media, three places, one off the premises;
  • the LOCKSS principle ("lots of copies keep stuff safe");
  • keep the important in several formats (for example, text as .docx, .pdf and .txt);
  • hand over copies and passwords in advance to the one who will continue the research.

What else is worth doing:

  • write a short note "how my archive is organized" and put it at the root of the folder;
  • tell relatives where the archive is (this is already in Keeping files);
  • FamilySearch Memories: photographs, documents, stories, audio linked to people in the tree. Only you see your own gallery; memories linked to the deceased are seen by all users [from search snippet]. This is both a backup and a way to share.

7. The family book

How to write a family history in 10 steps — Maegan Kasteler (FamilySearch blog, "Writing and Publishing a Family History: 10 Steps") [checked]:

  1. Remember: it is a story, not a report on the research.
  2. Define the reader and the period.
  3. Choose the format.
  4. Scope, outline, table of contents.
  5. Formatting rules.
  6. Write.
  7. Add narrative and illustrations.
  8. Reread and correct.
  9. Make an index of names.
  10. Publish.

Lisa A. Alzo (Writing Your Family History Book) advises starting with the ancestors about whom there is the most material [from search snippet].

How to write the text itself — Writing the family history.

8. Where to pass originals

  • Yad Vashem, the "Gathering the Fragments" programme (since 2011): they accept photographs, letters, documents, objects — as a gift or on temporary loan. Items are conserved, catalogued and digitised (Yad Vashem USA, "Share Your Documents and Artifacts") [checked].
  • The Central State Archive of Moscow includes the former Central Moscow Archive-Museum of Personal Collections and accepts family archives [from search snippet: cgamos.ru]. The procedure for transfer was not checked.
  • Before handing over, scan everything and keep a copy in the family. Record what, where and when was handed over, and the storage number if one is given.
