# Ideas for a family history: what else to look for and what to ask AI

What this section covers: how to turn dates and names into a living family history — the context of place and time, social estate and occupation, schooling, newspapers and courts, the history of a surname, namesakes, a migration map and a visual tree, a trip to your roots — and, at the end, films and books for inspiration.

> Examples from my own research are anonymized. Information about websites was checked on 17.10.2026.

---

## 1. A brief on the place and the time

Ask AI to put together a brief on the shtetl or town for the years your ancestors lived there:
- who owned the place (a private shtetl or a crown town), what suburbs it had;
- how many people lived there, how many Jews, which communities;
- how people made a living: crafts, factories, fairs, trade;
- events: wars, fires, epidemics, pogroms, expulsions;
- which uezd, guberniya and state it belonged to in each year (see [Places and maps](../2-reference/06-places-maps.md), section 4b).

**Example.** A Podolian shtetl turned out to be privately owned: in the 1780s the owner invited German cloth-makers and obtained a month-long fair, and by the end of the 19th century dozens of cloth factories worked there. Jews in the shtetl were workers, dyers and traders. This explained why the ancestors were registered as craftsmen — and where to look for their papers: not only in town books but in the estate's records.

**Where to look:** Słownik geograficzny Królestwa Polskiego (articles on shtetls, [University of Warsaw search](https://szukajwslownikach.uw.edu.pl/slownik-geograficzny/)), the [YIVO Encyclopedia](https://yivoencyclopedia.org/), the [Electronic Jewish Encyclopedia](https://eleven.co.il/) (in Russian), shtetl pages on [JewishGen KehilaLinks](https://kehilalinks.jewishgen.org/), community memorial books ([Yizkor Book Project](https://www.jewishgen.org/yizkor/)).

> "Put together a brief on [shtetl] in [years]: owners, population, Jews, economy, events, which state it belonged to in each year. A source for every fact."

## 2. Social estate and occupation

An estate is a status, not an origin: *meshchanin*, guild craftsman, merchant, honorary citizen. Statuses changed; a merchant guild was bought every year. Ask AI to explain what the status meant at the time and which documents it left behind: guild certificates, address calendars, the "Vsya Rossiya" directory (factories, shops), lists of physicians and pharmacists, craft boards.

> "What did it mean to be [a 2nd-guild merchant / a guild tailor] in [guberniya] in [years]? Which documents about it survive and where should I look?"

## 3. Schooling: gymnasiums and universities

- **Gymnasiums** printed annual reports with lists of pupils and the father's status.
- **Universities** published "Lichny sostav" — printed lists of students with patronymic and guberniya.
- **The Album Academicum** of Dorpat (Yuryev) University gives the date of birth, the gymnasium and the years of study.
- **The University of Vienna archive** ([PHAIDRA](https://phaidra.univie.ac.at/)) holds student forms (*Nationale*) with the father's name, occupation and place of residence.

Students' personal files are kept in national archives and are not digitised everywhere.

**Example.** An ancestor was born in the 1860s in southern Bessarabia. He finished the local gymnasium, studied medicine in Vienna for a year, then in Yuryev, and became a physician. The Vienna form named his father a merchant and gave the town. This one line settled a question that had stayed open for years.

> "My ancestor [name], born around [year], was a physician. Where can I find lists of medical students for [years] and their forms?"

## 4. Newspapers, obituaries, wills, courts

- **Newspapers.** The Jewish press of the 19th–20th centuries is digitised on the National Library of Israel site (Historical Jewish Press). Russian newspapers can be searched in Yandex "Search in archives" and the NEB. Look for authors' signatures, missing-person notices, obituaries and donor lists.
- **Wills and probate files** are in the records of district courts and notaries. They list the heirs by name.
- **Notarial and court files** show mortgages, auction sales and disputes. They show how a family grew rich and went bankrupt.
- **Directories:** "Vsya Rossiya", address calendars, city address and telephone books.

**Example.** An 1884 Jewish newspaper carried an article signed by a man with the same surname, with patronymic and town. An 1887 notice from a wife looking for her husband, who had left, gave names and kinship. The 1900 "Vsya Rossiya" directory listed an ancestor's three sawmills and the number of workers.

## 5. The history of a surname

A surname can have several explanations: from a word, a place, an occupation, the mother's name. Jews of the Russian Empire were given surnames in 1804–1830s, and the same surname arose independently in different places. So namesakes often turn out to be different families. You can check a surname in the index to Beider's dictionaries at [stevemorse.org](https://www.stevemorse.org/phonetics/beider.php): whether it exists and in which uezds. More in [Names and spellings](../2-reference/03-names-spellings.md).

> "What versions of the origin of the surname [X] exist? In which uezds was it found? What supports each version?"

## 6. Namesakes from one region

Put everyone with the surname from the guberniya into one table: who, where, when, the father's name, the family number in the revision, the occupation. The table shows where a connection is possible and where it is not. A connection is confirmed only by a second feature: place, patronymic, age, relatives. See [Assembling the whole family](../2-reference/09-whole-family.md).

> "Put all the [X] from [guberniya] for [years] into a table and suggest how they might be connected. Mark what is proven and what is a hypothesis."

## 7. A migration map and a visual tree

- **Map:** shtetl → city → evacuation → emigration, by year and by document. AI can make it from a "year — place — document" table.
- **A one-page tree:** a chart from a GEDCOM export or a table, to show relatives.

Use only verified data. If you send the tree to someone, remove living people.

> "Draw a map of the family's moves by year from this table. Label each point with its document."

## 8. Notable people of the place

Rabbis, teachers, physicians, merchants, writers — people your ancestors may have met. Look for them in YIVO, the Electronic Jewish Encyclopedia and memorial books. It helps you picture the community, and sometimes a direct link turns up: a teacher, a wedding witness, a business partner.

> "Who were the well-known rabbis, physicians and teachers of [shtetl] in the 19th century? What is known about them?"

## 9. A trip to your roots

In Israel, roots trips are popular: to the ancestors' shtetls and to Holocaust sites. Schoolchildren travel to Poland; families go to Ukraine, Moldova, Lithuania.

How to prepare:
- plan the route using the names and maps of the time;
- find the synagogue, the cemetery and the site of the house from the address in the documents;
- for Jewish cemeteries, use the JOWBR burial database on JewishGen and the [ESJF](https://esjf-cemeteries.org/) maps;
- read the shtetl's KehilaLinks page and the community memorial book;
- contact a local museum, archive, local historian or guide.

## 10. For inspiration: films, books, series

**Films:**
- "Everything Is Illuminated" (2005, dir. Liev Schreiber, from the novel by Jonathan Safran Foer) — an American searches Ukraine for the woman who saved his grandfather.
- "A Real Pain" (2024, Jesse Eisenberg, Kieran Culkin) — two cousins travel through Poland in their grandmother's footsteps.
- "Ida" (2013, Paweł Pawlikowski) — a novice nun learns she is Jewish and looks for her relatives' graves.
- "Lion" (2016) — 25 years later, a man finds his childhood home on satellite images.
- "Three Identical Strangers" (2018) — a documentary about triplets separated at birth.
- "Philomena" (2013) — a mother searches for her son 50 years later.

**Books:**
- Edmund de Waal, "The Hare with Amber Eyes" — a family history from Odessa to Vienna through a netsuke collection.
- Lyudmila Ulitskaya, "Jacob's Ladder" — a novel based on a family archive of letters.
- Daniel Mendelsohn, "The Lost: A Search for Six of Six Million" — a search for six relatives killed in the Holocaust.

**Series:** "Who Do You Think You Are?" (BBC) and "Finding Your Roots" (PBS). In each episode a well-known person discovers their family history — a good lesson in reading documents.

## 11. Seven tips to remember

1. Take citizenship rules only from the official site — and note the date ([Citizenship by descent](../2-reference/14-citizenship-by-descent.md)).
2. First draw the chain of generations, then look for documents.
3. Check a family legend with both a document and DNA ([DNA](../2-reference/15-dna.md)).
4. Search for a name in all its forms and languages: Moshe, Moishe, Moshko, Moszek, Musiy, Moisei, Max.
5. Search for a surname in all spellings — and without its first letter.
6. Search for a town under the name it had in the year of the event.
7. The index is a hint; the scan decides.
