# Standard of proof: when you can say "found"

What this section covers: how to evaluate sources, when a match becomes proof, what "not found" means, and how to record a conclusion so that another person can check it. Gathered here are rules worked out in practice and international standards: the Genealogical Proof Standard (BCG) and the approach of Elizabeth Shown Mills (Evidence Explained).

---

## 1. The hierarchy of sources

A rough ladder, from strong to weak:

1. **A scan of a primary document** (metrical book, revision list, ZAGS record) — look with your own eyes.
2. **A printed directory of the era** ("All Russia" — *Vsya Rossiya*, memorial booklets — *pamyatnye knizhki*, medical lists).
3. **A document kept by relatives** (certificate, passport, statement).
4. **An index, an indexed database** — a hint about where the document lies.
5. **Someone else's tree** — not a source but **someone else's hypothesis**.

The archive and the document are always above a record in a tree and above family memory.

**Encyclopedias and Wikipedia are not a source on origin.** The place of registration (*pripiska*) is passed off there as the place of birth, the number of children is sometimes wrong (those who died in infancy were not known), and an article in an old encyclopedia could have been paid for by the subject himself. Nadya Lipes, in an interview, estimated that the birthplaces of prominent Jews in encyclopedias are given wrongly in nearly 90% of cases (isrageo.com, 2023) — a practitioner's opinion, not a statistic. Check against metrical books, censuses, petitions.

This ladder matches Mills's division into **original**, **derivative** and **authored** sources. Mills explicitly classes quality images of originals as originals, and indexes and database records as derivatives.

⚠️ A caveat. The ladder ranks **carriers**, not every field within a document. For example, a ZAGS birth certificate in the hands of relatives is an original, often with primary information, while a directory is a derivative text. Therefore it is more exact to evaluate each document on three axes (next item), and to keep the ladder as a landmark.

## 2. Three axes of evaluation (Mills)

Mills separates three different things:

| Axis | Options | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** (carrier) | original · derivative · authored | Is this the document itself, a copy/index, or someone's retelling? |
| **Information** (who reported) | primary · secondary · undetermined | Was the informant himself a witness of the event? Or is he retelling? Or is it unknown who reported (as in almost all censuses)? |
| **Evidence** (in relation to the question) | direct · indirect · negative | Does the document answer the question directly, or can the answer only be inferred from it? |

Why this matters: **even a primary scan may contain secondary information.** The age and birthplace in a death record are reported by a relative, not by the deceased. The age in a revision list is often reported by the village elder. In an American death certificate the data on the parents is given by a relative-informant, and it is sometimes inaccurate.

A convenient note at each fact in the finds log: "orig. / secondary / indirect".

Source: Evidence Explained, QuickLesson 17 "Evidence Analysis Process Map" (evidenceexplained.com); a discussion on legacytree.com ("Evidence analysis: sources").

## 3. The Genealogical Proof Standard (GPS) — five elements

The proof standard of the Board for Certification of Genealogists (BCG). A conclusion is considered proven if all five conditions are met:

1. **Reasonably exhaustive research** — on the question, all sources that could have answered it have been viewed, including indirect ones and the person's circle.
2. **Complete and accurate source citations** — with archival reference, page, frame.
3. **Thorough analysis and correlation** — of each source on the three axes.
4. **Resolution of conflicting evidence** — every discrepancy is explained, not discarded.
5. **A soundly reasoned, written conclusion** based on the strongest evidence.

FamilySearch ("Brief Review of Research Standards") adds three criteria of proof: the evidence is sufficient, it relates to the question, it is reliable.

## 4. When you can say "found"

A practical rule that almost fully coincides with the GPS. "Found" only if:

1. **the search was reasonably complete** (not the first match that came along);
2. **the source is cited with its archival reference** and verbatim;
3. there is a **second independent match** — not only the surname but also the patronymic, age, place, occupation, names of relatives;
4. **contradictions have been explicitly resolved**.

A surname alone is not a match. A surname plus place may also be a coincidence of namesakes. Features are needed that together occur in only one person.

## 5. Independence of confirmations

For Mills, a confirmation counts only if the sources were **created independently** and do not go back to one original source.

- A JewishGen index and the scan of the same record are **one** piece of evidence, not two.
- Two trees on MyHeritage copied from each other are also one.
- Two extracts from one metrical record are one.
- MyHeritage "Smart Matches" from Geni, if the tree on Geni is your own, are your own data, not an independent source.

## 6. An index is not proof

Look at every record from a database on the scan. Real cases from one investigation:
- the index gave "Ester, daughter of Duvid" — in fact a field-parsing error: Duvid turned out to be the groom's father;
- the index read "Khuna, son of Abram" — by the scan Abram was the bride's father;
- «Рашковскій мещ.» (Rashkovsky mesh.) meant "meshchanin of the shtetl Rashkov", not a surname;
- a person is in the list at the frame but absent from the alphabetical index of the same section;
- in an American index "Chaim Shefel" turned out by the frame to be Stiefel;
- a naturalization card in the index gave 1938, while by the frame it was 1932.

Advice from the NGS (National Genealogical Society, USA): online materials are a hint, not ready-made proof; do not accept programs and online trees uncritically; an author's text is useful only if it has references to sources.

## 7. "Not found" — a negative search and negative evidence

Mills distinguishes two things:
- **A negative search** — simply "we did not find it".
- **Negative evidence** — a conclusion from the absence of something that **had to be there** (like the dog that did not bark in Sherlock Holmes).

An example: a male meshchanin of the right age must be in the revision of his society. If he is not — that is evidence (ran away, died, registered elsewhere), not just an empty search. But the absence of a wife in the revision of 1811 means nothing: that revision recorded only men.

**"Not found" has force only if the following are stated:**
1. **the source and its limits** — which file, which pages or frames were viewed;
2. **the list of spellings** searched;
3. **the method of search** — index, full-text search, page-by-page reading; exact or phonetic;
4. whether the **index or the document itself** was looked at.

And one more question: **was the person obliged to appear in this source?** It helps to ask **under what law the source was created**: this explains who is in it, who is not, and which columns to trust (the approach of the lawyer and genealogist Judy Russell) [from search snippet]. Historians remind us that the metrical books and censuses of the Russian Empire were incomplete, some people evaded being counted, and a gap in a metrical book proves nothing by itself (E. Avrutin, "Jews and the Imperial State", 2010) [from search snippet].

Add two more checks:
- **neighbours' books**: the records of a shtetl are sometimes bound into the books of a neighbouring rabbinate or the district town; before concluding "there are no books", check the neighbours;
- **date of the search**: databases grow (in 2026 the JewishGen Ukraine Research Division was adding 100–150 thousand records a month), so a negative result is valid only as of the date of the search — record it and repeat the search after additions.

### The control record

Before believing a zero, test the search on a **control record** — a word or name that is definitely in this volume (the name of the town, a known rare surname, a record from an index). Found — the zero is real. Not found — the search is broken, rather than the person absent.

A real case: a mass pass through newspaper issues returned "0 finds" because all the site's responses were access errors (403). Any mass pass must be checked on a known positive example.

### Gaps in the sources themselves

A gap is not always a failure of the search. Some books did not survive, some were not filmed, leaves are sometimes torn out of files (check — by a break in the numbering of folios). This must be recorded as a result: "the book for 1869 is not filmed in the catalogue", not "not found".

## 8. Written reasoning: three levels (BCG)

| Form | When | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Proof statement** | There is direct evidence, no contradictions | One sentence and an archival reference |
| **Proof summary** | Several pieces of evidence, minor contradictions | A point-by-point discussion |
| **Proof argument** | Evidence conflicts, or there is no direct evidence | A coherent text, tables, diagrams, sometimes several pages |

BCG stresses: the boundaries between the forms are blurred; what matters is not the name but the persuasiveness. Practical conclusion: **a hypothesis built on indirect data should, before the status "confirmed", be set out as a proof argument.** It is written as text: which evidence, why it is independent, what is against, why the alternatives were dropped.

Source: bcgcertification.org, "Ten-Minute Methodology: Proof Summaries and Arguments".

## 9. Searching the circle — the FAN principle

FAN — Friends, Associates, Neighbors. When there are no documents about the person himself, information about him is often found in the documents of his circle. People moved in groups, married within the circle, served as witnesses for each other.

Mills advises solving problems of people with the same name this way: build a network of related persons from official papers, limit the search circle to the real conditions of life (craft, distances), compare candidates across several types of documents. The MyHeritage Wiki ("Collateral and cluster genealogy research") adds: search by address, try different orders of names, do not rely only on indexes, check neighbouring uezds at the border.

Where to look for the circle:
- **witnesses and godparents/sponsors** in metrical books (in one investigation a "newborn's uncle" was found among the witnesses of a birth record);
- **guarantors and partners** in court and commercial files;
- **neighbouring families** in a revision;
- **fellow travellers** in ship manifests;
- **neighbours at an address** in directories like "All Moscow" (*Vsya Moskva*);
- **authors of correspondence** and donors in newspapers.

Set up a "circle" line for each key person.

**The birth order of children** is another independent confirmation. The order of seniority derived from the ages in one revision must coincide with the order in another. If it coincides — a strong argument that it is one family; if not — a reason to look for a second wife or a second family (the method of Chaim Friedman) [checked]. How to assemble a whole family and sort out namesakes — [Reconstructing the whole family](../2-reference/09-whole-family.md).

⚠️ The GPS requires exhaustive research **on the question**, including the circle. Closing a line of inquiry as "empty" without looking at the circle may be premature.

Sources: evidenceexplained.com, QuickLesson 11 "Identity Problems & the FAN Principle"; myheritage.com/wiki/Collateral_and_cluster_genealogy_research.

## 10. Checklists

**Before saying "found":**
- [ ] The scan is open and read with your own eyes (not only the index).
- [ ] The quotation is verbatim, the archival reference and frame are recorded.
- [ ] There is a second independent feature (not only the surname).
- [ ] The sources really are independent (not an index + the same scan).
- [ ] Contradictions are named and explained.
- [ ] If the conclusion is indirect — the reasoning is written.

**Before saying "no":**
- [ ] The source and the limits of what was viewed are named.
- [ ] All spellings are named.
- [ ] The method of search (index / full text / page by page) is named.
- [ ] The search was tested on a control record.
- [ ] It is clear whether the person had to be in this source.
- [ ] It was checked whether part of the source is lost or not filmed.
- [ ] The books of neighbouring rabbinates and the district town were checked.
- [ ] The date of the search is recorded (databases grow).
