Standard of proof: when you can say "found"

What this section covers: how to evaluate sources, when a match becomes proof, what "not found" means, and how to record a conclusion so that another person can check it. Gathered here are rules worked out in practice and international standards: the Genealogical Proof Standard (BCG) and the approach of Elizabeth Shown Mills (Evidence Explained).

1. The hierarchy of sources

A rough ladder, from strong to weak (with a caveat below):

  1. A primary document — a scan of an archival file (metrical book, revision list, ZAGS record) or an original of the time that survives with relatives (certificate, passport, statement), including a photo of it. An original in the hands of relatives is not lower than a scan. Look with your own eyes.
  2. A printed directory of the era ("All Russia" — Vsya Rossiya, memorial booklets — pamyatnye knizhki, medical lists) — a derivative text, but contemporary with the event.
  3. Later family records and stories — a hand-copied transcript, entries in an album or notebook, memoirs.
  4. An index, an indexed database — a hint about where the document lies.
  5. Someone else's tree — not a source but someone else's hypothesis.

The archive and the document are always above a record in a tree and above family memory.

Encyclopedias and Wikipedia are not a source on origin. The place of registration (pripiska) is passed off there as the place of birth, the number of children is sometimes wrong (those who died in infancy were not known), and an article in an old encyclopedia could have been paid for by the subject himself. Nadia Lipes, in an interview, estimated that the birthplaces of prominent Jews in encyclopedias are given wrongly in nearly 90% of cases (isrageo.com, 2023) — a practitioner's opinion, not a statistic. Check against metrical books, censuses, petitions.

This ladder matches Mills's division into original, derivative and authored sources. Mills explicitly classes quality images of originals as originals, and indexes and database records as derivatives.

⚠️ A caveat. The ladder ranks carriers, while it is the specific document and the specific field that decide. A ZAGS birth certificate in the hands of relatives is an original, often with primary information, so it stands next to a scan, not below a directory. And fields within one document are not equal: an age stated by the person himself is weaker than a date from a metrical record, although both stand in a primary document. So it is more exact to evaluate each document and each field on three axes (next item), and to keep the ladder as a landmark.

2. Three axes of evaluation (Mills)

Mills separates three different things:

Axis Options What to ask
Source (carrier) original · derivative · authored Is this the document itself, a copy/index, or someone's retelling?
Information (who reported) primary · secondary · undetermined Was the informant himself a witness of the event? Or is he retelling? Or is it unknown who reported (as in almost all censuses)?
Evidence (in relation to the question) direct · indirect · negative Does the document answer the question directly, or can the answer only be inferred from it?

Why this matters: even a primary scan may contain secondary information. The age and birthplace in a death record are reported by a relative, not by the deceased. The age in a revision list is often reported by the village elder. In an American death certificate the data on the parents is given by a relative-informant, and it is sometimes inaccurate.

A convenient note at each fact in the finds log: "orig. / secondary / indirect".

Source: Evidence Explained, QuickLesson 17 "Evidence Analysis Process Map" (evidenceexplained.com); a discussion on legacytree.com ("Evidence analysis: sources").

3. The Genealogical Proof Standard (GPS) — five elements

The proof standard of the Board for Certification of Genealogists (BCG). A conclusion is considered proven if all five conditions are met:

  1. Reasonably exhaustive research — on the question, all sources that could have answered it have been viewed, including indirect ones and the person's circle.
  2. Complete and accurate source citations — with archival reference, page, image.
  3. Thorough analysis and correlation — of each source on the three axes.
  4. Resolution of conflicting evidence — every discrepancy is explained, not discarded.
  5. A soundly reasoned, written conclusion based on the strongest evidence.

FamilySearch ("Brief Review of Research Standards") adds three criteria of proof: the evidence is sufficient, it relates to the question, it is reliable.

4. When you can say "found"

A practical rule that almost fully coincides with the GPS. "Found" only if:

  1. the search was reasonably complete (not the first match that came along);
  2. the source is cited with its archival reference and verbatim;
  3. there is a second independent match — not only the surname but also the patronymic, age, place, occupation, names of relatives;
  4. contradictions have been explicitly resolved.

A surname alone is not a match. A surname plus place may also be a coincidence of namesakes. Features are needed that together occur in only one person.

4a. The status scale

One scale for hypotheses, links between people and families, and agents' finds — throughout the guide, in the templates and in the skills:

Status What it means What is needed
🟢 confirmed proven a document read from the scan (the record directly names the fact or the relationship, or there is a bridge of ages between revisions), plus a second independent feature; contradictions resolved
🟡 open / strong version there are signs, there is no proof two or more independent indirect features, no contradictions; the reasoning is written down. Work is under way on it
⚪ unverified / weak a hint one feature: a match of surname and place, a line of an index or database without a scan, a family legend, someone else's tree
🔴 rejected refuted or dropped always with the reason and date: "rejected 12.10.2026 — by the scan the father is not Abram but Khaim"

Some skills and working files also use 💭 — a guess: a version with no feature at all, only reasoning. It is below ⚪ and never goes into a tree; it can be written as ⚪ marked "guess".

Rules:

  • A line of an index, database or finding aid is ⚪ until the document itself is opened. An index and the scan of the same record are one piece of evidence, not two.
  • A family legend is ⚪ with a note of who told it. Do not argue with it and do not discard it: check it.
  • What to put in a tree: in a publicly accessible online tree — only 🟢; in a personal working tree you may put 🟡 marked "version"; ⚪ and 🔴 — only in the hypotheses file.
  • The status is changed by a person, not by AI: AI proposes, the person decides.
  • Before moving 🟡 to 🟢 on indirect data, write a proof argument (section 8).

A register of family legends

Keep a separate file of legends: one line per claim, a status on the same scale, and what it was checked against. Break each legend into parts: "born in village N, she came from the Germans, her mother was a healer" is three claims with different statuses. Name and year may be 🟢 from a memorial book, the village ⚪, "from the Germans" 🔴 literally but 🟡 in the sense "non-Russian settlers". Do not delete refuted legends: a line "🔴 — refuted by …" saves time for the next person who hears the story.

Experience of one register (62 legends across four family lines): 5 fully confirmed, 18 partly, 19 refuted. Families remember the gist well (the navy, the evacuation, a doctor in the family, a link to another country) but mix up places, dates and names.

5. Independence of confirmations

For Mills, a confirmation counts only if the sources were created independently and do not go back to one original source.

  • A JewishGen index and the scan of the same record are one piece of evidence, not two.
  • Two trees on MyHeritage copied from each other are also one.
  • Two extracts from one metrical record are one.
  • MyHeritage "Smart Matches" from Geni, if the tree on Geni is your own, are your own data, not an independent source.

6. An index is not proof

Look at every record from a database on the scan. Real cases from one investigation:

  • the index gave "Ester, daughter of Duvid" — in fact a field-parsing error: Duvid turned out to be the groom's father;
  • the index read "Khuna, son of Abram" — by the scan Abram was the bride's father;
  • «Рашковскій мещ.» (Rashkovsky mesh.) meant "meshchanin of the shtetl Rashkov", not a surname;
  • a person is in the list on the image but absent from the alphabetical index of the same section;
  • in an American index "Chaim Feigel" turned out by the image to be Fiegel;
  • a naturalization card in the index gave 1938, while by the image it was 1932.

Advice from the NGS (National Genealogical Society, USA): online materials are a hint, not ready-made proof; do not accept programs and online trees uncritically; an author's text is useful only if it has references to sources.

7. "Not found" — a negative search and negative evidence

Mills distinguishes two things:

  • A negative search — simply "we did not find it".
  • Negative evidence — a conclusion from the absence of something that had to be there (like the dog that did not bark in Sherlock Holmes).

An example: a male meshchanin of the right age must be in the revision of his society. If he is not — that is evidence (ran away, died, registered elsewhere), not just an empty search. But the absence of a wife in the revision of 1811 means nothing: that revision recorded only men.

"Not found" has force only if the following are stated:

  1. the source and its limits — which file, which pages or images were viewed;
  2. the list of spellings searched;
  3. the method of search — index, full-text search, page-by-page reading; exact or phonetic;
  4. whether the index or the document itself was looked at.

And one more question: was the person obliged to appear in this source? It helps to ask under what law the source was created: this explains who is in it, who is not, and which columns to trust (the approach of the lawyer and genealogist Judy Russell) [from search snippet]. Historians remind us that the metrical books and censuses of the Russian Empire were incomplete, some people evaded being counted, and a gap in a metrical book proves nothing by itself (E. Avrutin, "Jews and the Imperial State", 2010) [from search snippet].

Add two more checks:

  • neighbours' books: the records of a shtetl are sometimes bound into the books of a neighbouring rabbinate or the district town; before concluding "there are no books", check the neighbours;
  • date of the search: databases grow (in 2026 the JewishGen Ukraine Research Division was adding 100–150 thousand records a month), so a negative result is valid only as of the date of the search — record it and repeat the search after additions.

The control record

Before believing a zero, test the search on a control record — a word or name that is definitely in this volume (the name of the town, a known rare surname, a record from an index). Found — the zero is real. Not found — the search is broken, rather than the person absent.

A real case: a mass pass through newspaper issues returned "0 finds" because all the site's responses were access errors (403). Any mass pass must be checked on a known positive example.

A zero can also be false without any site errors: a wrongly built query address, a different route to the same database, a search with synonyms that does not know your spelling, a catalogue that looks for all the words in one file title. A control record is needed for each route separately, and every zero needs the number looked at and a denominator ("118 of 245 households"). Details — False zero.

The denominator and what the source contains

Five observations from a reader of the guide on how to count coverage (October 2026, CC BY 4.0) [reader's experience]:

  1. The denominator is books and files, not calendar years. The author wrote down: "of the 21 years of the window, 4 have been read — coverage 19 %, the direction is open". The list of storage units showed that for these years exactly four books exist, and all of them have been read: coverage 100 %, the direction is exhausted. A record cannot hide in a year for which there is no book. Before announcing coverage, get the list of storage units and count by it. The reverse is also true: a year covered by a book is not necessarily covered by the right kind of record. In the author's corpus, over 39 years there were birth books for only 21 years; the other years were covered by marriage and death books (compare False zero, section 12).
  2. A file that names people is not necessarily yours. Of 19 files on one shtetl, six named people one by one, but the person sought could not be in them: they were Party lists of 1937–1945, and he had died earlier and was never a Party member. Before putting a file in the queue for reading or ordering, ask whom the source was obliged to include by the way it was built, and compare with your person's estate, religion and dates. This is the same question "under what law was the source created", only asked before reading.
  3. Look for a field that cuts off the wrong corpus mechanically. In the fond of one uezd police administration lay metrical books of two corpora: Jewish books and books of Old Believers and sectarians, whose records were kept by the police under the law of 1874. By their titles ("register of the dead", "register of the born") they cannot be told apart. The inventory's "language" field told them apart: in the Jewish series "Russian, Hebrew" stands in 117 entries of 117, in the neighbouring one in 0 of 17. The author nearly sent someone to the reading room for non-Jewish books, and in his own notes this file had been praised: "and it's in Russian, easy to read". The sign of trouble read as a merit. Language, religion, estate, the issuing authority can play this role: a check by such a field is cheaper than any check by content. A title works too: "register of the born and baptised" — the book is not Jewish.
  4. What to read on the fond's card.
    • The "other finding aids" field is sometimes an inventory of inventories. The portal showed 134 units for the fond, while the field listed seven inventories with their sizes: the fond has 1,129 units, the portal 11.9 %. Without it, "the inventory is fully copied" would have meant "one ninth is copied".
    • The "history of the fond" says what exactly has been put online. It said which inventories were entered into the system and when: metrical books of Jews and sectarians. So these 11.9 % are not a random fragment but the right layer: a zero inside it is meaningful, and outside it the record could not have been.
    • A series' outer dates do not say what records it holds. The series "rabbinate registers, 1867–1869" consisted of two units: births of 1867 and deaths of 1869. There were no births of 1869 in it.
    • The search results line truncates the size field just as it truncates the archival reference: "575" turned out to be the start of "575 + 48 = 623".
  5. Filter by all the names of the issuing body, not by the place name. The author declared the list of a shtetl's books closed at 22 units — there were 23. The twenty-third lay in a series under the name of a neighbouring shtetl: one rabbinate served three shtetls and appeared in the inventory under three names. The line had been in the already-copied inventory all along — the filter failed. A sign that there are several names: compound names with a hyphen in the series titles of one fond. Look for a missing book under the neighbouring shtetl (see "neighbours' books" above).

How to check that a file on a website is the right one and has been read in full (cover, certification sheet, full list of images) — Checking results, section 2a.

Gaps in the sources themselves

A gap is not always a failure of the search. Some books did not survive, some were not filmed, leaves are sometimes torn out of files (check — by a break in the numbering of folios). This must be recorded as a result: "the book for 1869 is not filmed in the catalogue", not "not found".

8. Written reasoning: three levels (BCG)

Form When What it looks like
Proof statement There is direct evidence, no contradictions One sentence and an archival reference
Proof summary Several pieces of evidence, minor contradictions A point-by-point discussion
Proof argument Evidence conflicts, or there is no direct evidence A coherent text, tables, diagrams, sometimes several pages

BCG stresses: the boundaries between the forms are blurred; what matters is not the name but the persuasiveness. Practical conclusion: a hypothesis built on indirect data should, before the status 🟢 "confirmed", be set out as a proof argument. It is written as text: which evidence, why it is independent, what is against, why the alternatives were dropped.

Source: bcgcertification.org, "Ten-Minute Methodology: Proof Summaries and Arguments".

9. Searching the circle — the FAN principle

FAN — Friends, Associates, Neighbors. When there are no documents about the person himself, information about him is often found in the documents of his circle. People moved in groups, married within the circle, served as witnesses for each other.

Mills advises solving problems of people with the same name this way: build a network of related persons from official papers, limit the search circle to the real conditions of life (craft, distances), compare candidates across several types of documents. The MyHeritage Wiki ("Collateral and cluster genealogy research") adds: search by address, try different orders of names, do not rely only on indexes, check neighbouring uezds at the border.

Where to look for the circle:

  • witnesses and godparents/sponsors in metrical books (in one investigation a "newborn's uncle" was found among the witnesses of a birth record);
  • guarantors and partners in court and commercial files;
  • neighbouring families in a revision;
  • fellow travellers in ship manifests;
  • neighbours at an address in directories like "All Moscow" (Vsya Moskva);
  • authors of correspondence and donors in newspapers.

Set up a "circle" line for each key person.

The birth order of children is another independent confirmation. The order of seniority derived from the ages in one revision must coincide with the order in another. If it coincides — a strong argument that it is one family; if not — a reason to look for a second wife or a second family (the method of Chaim Friedman) [checked]. How to assemble a whole family and sort out namesakes — Reconstructing the whole family.

⚠️ The GPS requires exhaustive research on the question, including the circle. Closing a line of inquiry as "empty" without looking at the circle may be premature.

Sources: evidenceexplained.com, QuickLesson 11 "Identity Problems & the FAN Principle"; myheritage.com/wiki/Collateral_and_cluster_genealogy_research.

10. Checklists

Before saying "found":

  • [ ] The scan is open and read with your own eyes (not only the index).
  • [ ] The quotation is verbatim, the archival reference and image are recorded.
  • [ ] There is a second independent feature (not only the surname).
  • [ ] The sources really are independent (not an index + the same scan).
  • [ ] Contradictions are named and explained.
  • [ ] If the conclusion is indirect — the reasoning is written.

Before saying "no":

  • [ ] The source and the limits of what was viewed are named.
  • [ ] All spellings are named.
  • [ ] The method of search (index / full text / page by page) is named.
  • [ ] The search was tested on a control record — by the same route.
  • [ ] It is recorded how much was looked at and out of how many; the instrument was checked as working (False zero).
  • [ ] The denominator is taken from the list of storage units (books, files), not from the number of years; the zero was repeated after a pause.
  • [ ] It is clear whether the person had to be in this source.
  • [ ] It was checked whether part of the source is lost or not filmed.
  • [ ] The books of neighbouring parishes or rabbinates and the district town were checked.
  • [ ] The date of the search is recorded (databases grow).
