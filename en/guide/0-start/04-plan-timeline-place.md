# Research plan, a person's timeline, a place guide

What this section covers: how to work to a plan instead of dashing between sites. How to compile a timeline of a person and a family and catch "the impossible" in it, check it against the history of the locality, keep a guide for each shtetl, and study the whole family rather than one ancestor.

## 1. Order of work: timeline and place first, then the plan

Diana Elder and Nicole Dyer in the book *Research Like a Pro* propose this sequence [page read: familylocket.com]:

1. the goal (one question);
2. the timeline and its analysis;
3. a place (locality) guide;
4. the research plan;
5. formatting of source citations;
6. the research log;
7. the report.

What matters here is the order. The timeline and the place guide are compiled **before** the plan, not after. Then the plan rests on what is already known and on which documents exist for this place at all.

How to formulate the question — in [Where to start](02-where-to-start.md), section 5. The research log — in [Keeping files](../3-results/01-keeping-files.md) and the [template](../../templates/research-log.md).

## 2. The research plan: six steps

The model comes from the International Institute of Genealogical Studies (IIGS: Louise St Denis, Brenda Dougall Merriman, Penelope Christensen), on the FamilySearch Wiki page "Research Plan Example (National Institute)" [checked]. Each question gets its own plan:

1. **What is known.** Briefly, with references to documents.
2. **The goal.** One specific question.
3. **Analysis of what has been gathered.** How reliable each document is (see [Standard of proof](../3-results/02-standard-of-proof.md)).
4. **Working hypothesis.** What you expect to find.
5. **Sources by priority.** First those most likely to answer the question.
6. **Revision after finds.** The plan changes when new information appears.

Two reminders from the same page. Information at third hand is "information", not facts. Finds on the internet are only leads.

A ready plan can be written directly into the hypotheses file ([template](../../templates/hypothesis.md)): the "How to check" column is exactly item 5.

Thomas Jones's book *Mastering Genealogical Proof* is often recommended on this subject. In the search results only a table of contents by GPS elements is visible; no concrete advice on plans and timelines could be found [from search snippet: ngsgenealogy.org].

## 3. The timeline of a person and a family

**What it is.** A table of life events by date: not only births, marriages and deaths, but also censuses, revisions, service, transactions, moves, addresses from directories, mentions in newspapers. Each row has a source.

The FamilySearch Wiki advises keeping a timeline for each family, not only for one person ("US Timelines — Creation and Use with Families", a video by Angela McComas) [checked]. Amy Johnson Crow calls the timeline a "Swiss army knife" of genealogy: with it you weigh the evidence, find gaps and get new leads [page read: description of the talk on amyjohnsoncrow.com; the lecture itself was not found in open access].

### Checking for the impossible

When events are lined up in order, mistakes are visible at once. What to check (FamilySearch Wiki, same place):
- **the mother's age** at the births of the children: too young or too old;
- **intervals between children**: a suspiciously large gap may mean a child you have not yet found, or another wife;
- **a child born** more than nine months **after the father's death**. So people or dates have been mixed up [from search snippet: a digest of advice on typical mistakes];
- a person "simultaneously" in two places;
- ages in different documents diverging by more than a few years.

The impossible is not a reason to throw a document out. It is a reason to check whether there are two people here with the same name (see [Scenarios: what to do if…](../1-ai-search/07-scenarios.md), B2).

### Age in old documents

Age in revisions, censuses and questionnaires is often rounded or given "as of the last revision". In the timeline record it **as in the document** and next to it the calculated year of birth. A spread of 2–5 years between documents is usual. Details — the `tracing-revision-records` skill in [../../../skills/](../../../skills/).

## 4. Checking against the chronology of the locality

A family timeline is read together with the history of the place. Many "oddities" are explained by an event of the era rather than by a mistake. The FamilySearch Wiki advises checking the family's scale against wars and laws [checked].

For a Jewish family in the Pale of Settlement it is useful to keep these dates at hand (all of them are already covered in other sections of the guide):

| Year | Event | What it means for the search |
|---|---|---|
| 1795–1858 | revisions | the "ladder" of generations ([Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](../2-reference/01-records-empire-ussr.md)) |
| 1804 | the law obliging Jews to have surnames | earlier there may be no surname ([Names, surnames, spellings](../2-reference/03-names-spellings.md)) |
| 1827–1856 | recruitment of Jews, cantonists | a boy "disappears" from the family ([Special groups and name changes](../2-reference/04-special-groups-name-changes.md)) |
| 1835 | the Statute on Jews obliged rabbis to keep metrical books ("crown rabbi" as an office — from 1857) | from this time look for rabbinate fonds ([Jewish genealogy](../2-reference/02-jewish-genealogy.md)) |
| 1844 | abolition of the kahals | documents are grouped by "societies" ([Jewish genealogy](../2-reference/02-jewish-genealogy.md)) |
| 1856–1878 | southern Bessarabia part of Romania | no Russian metrical books ([Jewish genealogy](../2-reference/02-jewish-genealogy.md)) |
| 1918 | switch to the Gregorian calendar | dates shift by 13 days ([Handwriting, transliteration, dates](../2-reference/05-handwriting-transliteration-dates.md)) |
| 1919 | pogroms in Ukraine | deaths and family moves ([Jewish genealogy](../2-reference/02-jewish-genealogy.md)) |
| from 1924 | change of names and surnames by announcement | Khaim becomes Yefim ([Special groups and name changes](../2-reference/04-special-groups-name-changes.md)) |
| 1941 | evacuation, occupation | evacuation cards, victim lists ([War, repression, the siege](../2-reference/12-war-repression-siege.md)) |

Add your own local events: a fire in the shtetl, a change of uezd, the closing of a synagogue, the arrival of the railway. They are taken from the place guide.

## 5. The place guide (locality guide)

**One file for each shtetl or uezd.** So advise Legacy Tree Genealogists ("How to Create Locality Guides for Genealogy Research") [checked]. What it contains:

- **a table of contents with links** to sections;
- **maps** (each at once with a link to its source);
- **changes of subordination and names**: which uezd, guberniya, state the place belonged to in different years;
- **a brief history**, including the loss of documents (fires, wars, removal of archives);
- **which documents exist** — online and offline, for what years, where they are held;
- **archives** with addresses and fond references;
- **the date of last update**.

Additions from Lisa Stokes ("Research Like a Pro: Locality Research") [checked]:
- limit the guide to **the years you need**, not the whole history of the place;
- put **links to ready reference works** rather than rewriting them;
- put a link to the source beside each map at once, or in a month you will not find it.

Where to get information for the guide:
- what survives — Routes to Roots, the "Consolidated Catalog of Metrical Books" (*Zvedenyi katalog metrychnykh knyh*, Ukrainian), archive inventories ([Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](../2-reference/01-records-empire-ussr.md), section 8);
- names and subordination — gazetteers and maps ([Places and maps](../2-reference/06-places-maps.md));
- history — memorial books, encyclopedias ([Dictionaries, encyclopedias, reference works](../../reading/dictionaries-and-encyclopedias.md)).

The guide later turns into a "by place" chapter in the family history ([Writing the family history](../3-results/03-family-history.md), section 4).

## 6. Study the whole family, not one ancestor

- **One family at a time.** Crista Cowan (Ancestry's corporate genealogist, "The Barefoot Genealogist") says that jumping around the tree confuses and muddles most of all [page read: organizeyourfamilyhistory.com]. Bring one family to a report, then move on to the next.
- **What to write in the notes on a person** (same place): transcriptions of documents, **unsuccessful searches**, a to-do list and evidentiary reasoning. A source for every piece of information, even if it is "Grandmother said".
- **Brothers, sisters, brothers-in-law, witnesses.** A document about your ancestor is often found in the file of his brother or in the record of his sister's wedding. Studying a person apart from family and neighbours is a typical beginner mistake (FamilySearch Wiki, "Rookie Mistakes") [checked]. Searching the circle is the FAN principle in [Standard of proof](../3-results/02-standard-of-proof.md), section 9.
- Keep the timeline **per family**: the events of all the children in one table at once show where the family lived in which years.
