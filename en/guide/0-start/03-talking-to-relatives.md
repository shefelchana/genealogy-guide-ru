# Talking to relatives

What this section covers: how to interview older relatives so as to get more than "I don't remember". How to prepare, what questions to ask, how to talk about the war and the Holocaust, how to record the conversation and afterwards check what was said against documents.

Markers: **[checked]** — the page was read; **[from search snippet]** — information from a search results snippet, the page was not opened.

Why it is urgent: not interviewing living relatives while it is still possible is one of the typical beginner mistakes (petergen.com, "Genealogy mistakes", Russian-language; the VGD forum, Russian-language) [checked]. What to do with a story afterwards — record it as a hypothesis, not as a fact ([Where to start](02-where-to-start.md), section 2).

## 1. Preparation

- **Meet in person if you can.** Oksana Korneva (vseopredkah.ru, okorneva.ru — Russian-language sites) writes that a personal meeting gives much more than a letter or a questionnaire: people are reluctant to write [checked].
- **Send the questions in advance** (American Ancestors, NEHGS, "Conducting a Family History Interview") [checked]. The person will have time to remember and to find papers.
- **Bring a ready-made chart of the relatives** and ask them to correct it. People answer more willingly than "from scratch" (Korneva) [checked].
- **Bring photographs and objects** as memory prompts (NEHGS) [checked] and a portable scanner, to digitize at once what they show you (Project Zhizn, the "History of You" method — Russian-language) [checked].
- **Adjust the length of the conversation** to the storyteller's health (NEHGS) [checked]. Two short meetings are better than one exhausting one.
- **Separate two aims**: a genealogical conversation (dates, marriages, moves, documents, photographs) and a biographical one (character, events, for memoirs). So advises the Am HaZikaron institute (amhazikaron.org) [checked].
- **Interview distant relatives too.** In Am HaZikaron's experience, women usually remember more family stories, and the most valuable information is often kept by very distant relatives. Discrepancies between stories are leads: record them rather than smoothing them over.

## 2. What questions to ask

### Time — through events, not years

The question "in what year?" usually brings "I don't remember". Ask about neighbouring events:
- "Was that before the wedding or after?"
- "Before your father died or after?"
- "Before the war? In evacuation? When did you come back?"
- "How old were you? What grade were you in?"

So advise guides to oral history, including the Smithsonian Center for Folklife [from search snippet: genealogy.com, nmaahc.si.edu — pages did not open, 403]. Afterwards the year is reconstructed from documents using such anchors. Enter the answers into the timeline at once ([Plan, timeline, place guide](04-plan-timeline-place.md)).

### Open questions

Questions that cannot be answered "yes" or "no" (same place [from search snippet]; VGD, docs.vgd.ru/kak-najti-predkov [checked]):
- "Tell me how people lived in the shtetl."
- "What was Grandmother like? What did she do?"
- "What holidays did you keep? What did you cook?"
- "Why did the family move?"

### What to be sure to ask

- **Places**: where they were born, where they came from, what the shtetl was called at home (it may differ from the official name).
- **Names**: what they were called at home, in documents, in the synagogue. What the storyteller's parents and brothers and sisters were called (see [Special groups and name changes](../2-reference/04-special-groups-name-changes.md)).
- **Occupations**, education, service.
- **Relatives abroad**: who left, where to, when, whether they corresponded.
- **Documents**: what is kept at home or by other relatives, who may know more.
- **Family legends** — record them too, even if they sound implausible (Korneva) [checked].

### During the conversation

- Ask **follow-up questions outside the list** and **allow pauses** — the person needs time to remember (NEHGS) [checked].
- **A photograph as a reason to ask**: "who is next to Grandmother?", "where was this taken?" ("History of You") [checked]. How to date the picture afterwards — [Old photographs](../2-reference/17-photographs.md).
- **Handle difficult subjects gently** (NEHGS) [checked]. If the person does not want to talk, do not insist.

## 3. Talking about the war, evacuation, the Holocaust

The model is the interview scheme of the USC Shoah Foundation [page read: sfi.usc.edu/content/interview]:

1. **A preliminary meeting** about a week before the recording and a detailed biographical questionnaire. This builds trust.
2. **The story in order**: life **before** the events, **during**, **after**.
3. **At the end** the storyteller shows photographs and documents and explains who is in them.
4. **A copy of the recording is given to the storyteller.**

If the finds turn out to be hard — see [Ethics and preservation](../3-results/04-ethics-preservation.md), section 3.

Irina Shcherbakova (Memorial) recorded oral memoirs from the late 1970s and ran the "Man in History" competition. Her methodological guides are mentioned, but could not be found in open access [from search snippet].

## 4. How to record

- **Record on a voice recorder or phone** (with the storyteller's consent). If you cannot meet, record a video or phone conversation too ("History of You") [checked].
- **Note at once**: the date of the conversation, who told it, who recorded it (Korneva) [checked].
- **Enter into a table** the full name, years, place and source number — immediately after the conversation. The paternal and maternal lines go in different folders (VGD) [checked].
- **Verbatim**, with the note "so says …" — especially names and place names as the storyteller pronounces them.
- Give the storyteller a copy of the recording or transcript (USC Shoah) [checked].
- Store the recording file by the rules in [Ethics and preservation](../3-results/04-ethics-preservation.md), section 6.

## 5. How to check what was said

A story is a hypothesis. Checking:

1. **Extract the checkable statements** one by one: "Grandfather was born in Zhitomir", "great-grandfather was a doctor", "the family left in 1922".
2. **Tie them to the timeline.** Does the story contradict documents already found? "The impossible" is visible at once ([Plan, timeline, place guide](04-plan-timeline-place.md), section 3).
3. **Pick a document** that will confirm or refute the statement (occupation — directories; a move — address books, questionnaires; war — the databases in [War, repression, the siege](../2-reference/12-war-repression-siege.md)).
4. **Compare the stories of different relatives.** If two people independently say the same thing, that is stronger. If one retells the other, that is one piece of evidence (see [Standard of proof](../3-results/02-standard-of-proof.md), section 5).
5. **Look for the "grain of truth".** Family memory confuses generations, years and names, but often preserves a correct detail: a place, an occupation, relatives abroad.

Specialists argue about legends. At the Am HaZikaron institute they consider that the most improbable legend often turns out to be true; Nadya Lipes writes that family legends almost always fail to be confirmed, especially those about origin. The practical conclusion in the middle: a legend is a hypothesis; it is neither discarded nor accepted but checked against a document.

> **How to ask AI**
>
> - "Here is a transcript of a conversation with [who] [text]. List the checkable statements one by one, noting who says so; for each, say which document could confirm or refute it." *Check yourself:* the AI did not add details that were not in the story.
> - "Help me draft questions for a conversation with [who, age]: through events rather than years; about places, occupations, the war, relatives abroad." *Check yourself:* the questions suit this person and do not touch painful subjects unnecessarily.
