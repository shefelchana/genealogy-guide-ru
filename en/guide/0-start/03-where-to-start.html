Where to start: the first two weeks

What this section covers: what to do at the very beginning, while nothing has been found yet. How to gather the home archive, ask your relatives and choose a first question. The one-page short version is the Quick start.

1. Gather everything you have at home

Before opening any sites, gather what is already in the family:

  • photographs, especially those with captions on the back (who, where, when);
  • documents: birth, marriage and death certificates, passports, work record books, military IDs, diplomas, evacuation certificates, death notices from the front;
  • letters and postcards: they often carry addresses, dates and names;
  • notebooks, albums, certificates of merit, newspaper clippings.

Photograph or scan every item. Write down at once, next to it, who is in the photo and where the document came from. In a year there may be no one left to tell you.

How to handle originals (advice of the VGD forum, docs.vgd.ru/kak-najti-predkov): do not restore them yourself, do not glue them into albums, and write on the back of a photo in pencil who, where and when.

2. Write down family memory — as hypotheses, not facts

Ask the older relatives. Write down word for word, with a note of who remembers it that way and when they told it: "so says Aunt N., conversation of 05.03.2026". The VGD forum advises also recording the circumstances of the telling.

Family memory often errs in dates, mixes up generations and changes surnames. But it sometimes holds precise leads. In one real piece of research, the phrase "we had relatives in Iași" led to Bessarabia, and documents were found there.

What they remember about the following is especially valuable:

  • towns and shtetls (where they were born, where they moved from);
  • occupations (doctor, merchant, teacher, timber dealer — there are separate directories for them);
  • wars, evacuation, repression;
  • relatives abroad (emigrant documents often name the shtetl and the parents).

If people of the older generation are alive, talking to them is the first item of the plan: it cannot be put off (so the Am HaZikaron institute holds). Ask very distant relatives too — they often keep what close ones do not have.

Every such statement is a hypothesis. It must be checked against a document. How to do that — in the section Standard of proof. How to prepare for the conversation, which questions to ask and how to record — Talking to relatives.

3. From the known to the unknown, one generation at a time

This is the common advice of all the guides: the JewishGen FAQ ("work from the known to the unknown, one small step at a time"), FamilySearch ("Research Process"), the VGD forum.

First establish reliably the parents of the person you know. Then his or her grandparents. Do not jump straight into the 18th century to a "famous ancestor" with the same surname: without the intermediate links that is not kinship but coincidence.

4. The main unit of search is the place, not the surname

The shtetl, uezd and guberniya determine which archive keeps the documents and which documents exist at all. Knowing only the country is not enough: you need the uezd and the guberniya (so FamilySearch Wiki writes about Ukraine, Belarus and Moldova).

What to do:

  1. Find the birthplace of the oldest person about whom there is a document (a passport, a certificate, a questionnaire, an evacuation card).
  2. Establish what the place was called then and which uezd and guberniya it belonged to. For this there are gazetteers: the JewishGen Gazetteer and Communities Database, for Belarus — Radzima, for Bessarabia — the Bessarabia Geographical Dictionary, and "Lists of Populated Places of the Russian Empire" (Списки населённых мест Российской империи). More — in Directories and books.
  3. Check what has survived for this place and where. The order is in Archives by country.

A surname is written in different ways, is recognised with errors, and before the 19th century among Jews often was not there at all (see Names, surnames, spellings). The place is more reliable.

5. One question at a time

Before every visit to the sites, formulate one specific question and what will count as an answer.

  • Bad: "find out more about my great-grandfather".
  • Good: "find the date of the marriage of my great-grandfather and great-grandmother" (a FamilySearch example, "Research Process").
  • Good: "who were the parents of Yakov, born in the 1860s in [town] in southern Bessarabia?"

Advice of the Board for Certification of Genealogists (BCG, the article "Focused versus Diffuse Research"): without a narrow question, neither analysis, nor comparison, nor proof is possible. Nadia Lipes also advises formulating the aim of the search clearly in advance, so as not to be disappointed afterwards.

How to turn a question into a six-step plan and compile a person's timeline — Plan, timeline, place guide.

Decide in advance when to stop: "no more than three evenings on this question" or "until I have viewed the whole 1868 birth register". So BCG recommends ("Planning Effective Research"): a plan is ended when the question is solved, the means are exhausted, or the time and money set aside have run out.

6. Where to start the search if you have only names

The most accessible layer is the Soviet and military one, which is closest in time:

  • military databases ("Pamyat naroda", OBD "Memorial");
  • Yad Vashem (evacuation cards, Pages of Testimony);
  • memorial books, "Otkrytyi spisok" (Open List);
  • scans of ZAGS registers of the 1920s–1940s (for Ukraine, part is posted on Wikimedia Commons);
  • cemetery databases.

Details — War, repression, the siege. From the oldest person with a known place, take a step back: the metrical books, censuses and revisions of that place (Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR).

If the family has an emigrant branch (the USA, Argentina, Palestine), start with it: American documents often name the shtetl directly. See Emigration.

7. Typical beginner mistakes

According to the observations of the VGD forum:

  • information is not systematised, sources are not given;
  • the hope that all the relatives will get enthusiastic (according to VGD, about a fifth are interested);
  • trying to do everything alone — yet there are forums, volunteers, groups (see Groups and communities);
  • no double-checking of facts.

To this it is worth adding:

  • entering everything into an online tree, guesses included;
  • not writing down where you have already searched — and a month later searching the same place a second time;
  • paying for a "genealogical search" what lies in open access (see Access, captchas, pace).

The result of the first two weeks

A table "person — dates — place — source" for everyone about whom something is known, plus one formulated question for the first round of searching. Lay the events of the period over the table — wars, pogroms, evacuation, changes of borders: some legends will at once stop agreeing on dates, and it will be clear which documents are missing (advice of N. Lipes).
