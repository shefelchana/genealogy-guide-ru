# Research plan, a person's timeline, a place guide

What this section covers: how to work to a plan rather than dart between sites. How to compile a timeline of a person and a family and catch the "impossible" in it, check it against the history of the locality, set up a guide for each shtetl, and study the whole family rather than one ancestor.

The marks in square brackets ([checked], [from search snippet], [unverified] and others) are explained in the [Glossary](06-glossary.md).

---

## 1. The order of work: timeline and place first, then the plan

Diana Elder and Nicole Dyer in the book *Research Like a Pro* propose this sequence [checked: familylocket.com]:

1. the aim (one question);
2. the timeline and its analysis;
3. the place guide;
4. the research plan;
5. formatting the source citations;
6. the research log;
7. the report.

What matters here is the order. The timeline and the place guide are made **before** the plan, not after. Then the plan rests on what is already known and on which documents exist for this place at all.

How to formulate the question — in [Where to start](03-where-to-start.md), section 5. The research log — in [Keeping files](02-keeping-files.md) and the [template](../../templates/research-log.md).

## 2. The research plan: six steps

The model is that of the National Institute for Genealogical Studies (IIGS: Louise St Denis, Brenda Dougall Merriman, Penelope Christensen) on FamilySearch Wiki, the page "Research Plan Example (National Institute)" [checked]. Each question gets its own plan:

1. **What is known.** Briefly, with links to documents.
2. **The aim.** One specific question.
3. **Analysis of what has been gathered.** How reliable each document is (see [Standard of proof](../3-results/01-standard-of-proof.md)).
4. **The working hypothesis.** What you expect to find.
5. **Sources by priority.** First those most likely to answer the question.
6. **Revision after finds.** The plan changes when new information appears.

Two reminders from the same place. Information from the words of third parties is "information", not facts. Finds on the internet are only leads.

A ready plan can be written straight into the hypotheses file ([template](../../templates/hypothesis.md)): the "How to check" field is step 5.

Thomas Jones's book *Mastering Genealogical Proof* is often recommended on this subject. In the search results only the table of contents, arranged by the elements of the GPS, was visible; the compiler did not come across concrete advice on a plan and a timeline [from search snippet: ngsgenealogy.org].

## 3. The timeline of a person and a family

**What it is.** A table of the events of a life by date: not only births, marriages and deaths, but also censuses, revisions, service, transactions, moves, addresses from directories, mentions in newspapers. Every row has a source.

FamilySearch Wiki advises keeping a timeline for each family, not only for one person ("US Timelines — Creation and Use with Families", a video by Angela McComas) [checked]. Amy Johnson Crow calls the timeline a "Swiss army knife" of genealogy: with it you weigh evidence, find gaps and get new leads [checked: the description of the talk on amyjohnsoncrow.com; the lecture itself was not found in open access].

### The check for the impossible

When events are lined up in order, errors are visible at once. What to check (FamilySearch Wiki, same place):
- **the mother's age** at the births of the children: too young or too old;
- **gaps between children**: a suspiciously large gap may mean a child you have not yet found, or another wife;
- **a child born more than nine months after the father's death**. That means people or dates have been mixed up [from search snippet: a summary of advice on typical mistakes];
- a person "simultaneously" in two places;
- ages in different documents differing by more than a few years.

The impossible is not a reason to throw a document out. It is a reason to check whether there are two people here with the same name (see [Scenarios: what to do if…](../1-ai-search/07-scenarios.md), B2).

### Age in old documents

Age in revisions, censuses and questionnaires is often rounded or given "as of the last revision". In the timeline write it **as in the document** and next to it the calculated birth year. A spread of 2–5 years between documents is usual. Details — the skill [`tracing-revision-records`](../../../skills/tracing-revision-records/SKILL.md) (in Russian).

## 4. Checking against the timeline of the locality

The family timeline is read together with the history of the place. Many "oddities" are explained by an event of the period, not by a mistake. FamilySearch Wiki advises checking the family's scale against wars and laws [checked].

For a Jewish family in the Pale of Settlement it is useful to keep these dates at hand (all of them are already discussed in other sections of the guide):

| Year | Event | What it means for the search |
|---|---|---|
| 1795–1858 | revisions | the "ladder" of generations ([Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](../2-reference/01-records-empire-ussr.md)) |
| 1804 | the law obliging Jews to have surnames | earlier there may be no surname ([Names, surnames, spellings](../2-reference/03-names-spellings.md)) |
| 1827–1856 | Jewish conscription, cantonists | a boy "disappears" from the family ([Special groups and name changes](../2-reference/04-special-groups-name-changes.md)) |
| 1835 | the Statute on Jews obliged rabbis to keep metrical books ("crown rabbi" (*kazyonny ravvin*) as an office — from 1857) | from then on look for rabbinate fonds ([Jewish genealogy](../2-reference/02-jewish-genealogy.md)) |
| 1844 | abolition of the kahals | documents are grouped by "societies" ([Jewish genealogy](../2-reference/02-jewish-genealogy.md)) |
| 1856–1878 | southern Bessarabia part of Romania | there is no Russian metrical book ([Jewish genealogy](../2-reference/02-jewish-genealogy.md)) |
| 1918 | the switch to the Gregorian calendar | dates shift by 13 days ([Handwriting, transliteration, dates](../2-reference/05-handwriting-transliteration-dates.md)) |
| 1919 | pogroms in Ukraine | deaths and families moving ([Jewish genealogy](../2-reference/02-jewish-genealogy.md)) |
| from 1924 | change of names and surnames by announcement | Khaim becomes Yefim ([Special groups and name changes](../2-reference/04-special-groups-name-changes.md)) |
| 1941 | evacuation, occupation | evacuation cards, lists of victims ([War, repression, the siege](../2-reference/12-war-repression-siege.md)) |

Add your own local events: a fire in the shtetl, a change of uezd, the closing of the synagogue, the arrival of the railway. They are taken from the place guide.

## 5. The place guide (locality guide)

**One file for each shtetl or uezd.** So advise Legacy Tree Genealogists ("How to Create Locality Guides for Genealogy Research") [checked]. What is in it:

- **a table of contents with links** to sections;
- **maps** (each at once with a link to its source);
- **changes of subordination and names**: which uezd, guberniya or state the place belonged to in different years;
- **a short history**, including the loss of documents (fires, wars, removal of archives);
- **which documents exist** — online and offline, for which years, where they are kept;
- **archives** with addresses and fond references;
- **the date of the last update**.

Additions from Lisa Stokes ("Research Like a Pro: Locality Research") [checked]:
- limit the guide to **the years you need**, not the whole history of the place;
- put **links to ready-made reference works** rather than rewriting them;
- put the link to the source beside every map at once, or in a month you will not find it.

Where to get information for the guide:
- what has survived — Routes to Roots, the "Consolidated catalog of metrical books" (Зведений каталог метричних книг), archive inventories ([Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](../2-reference/01-records-empire-ussr.md), section 8);
- names and subordination — gazetteers and maps ([Places and maps](../2-reference/06-places-maps.md));
- history — memorial books, encyclopedias ([Dictionaries and encyclopedias](../../reading/dictionaries-and-encyclopedias.md)).

The guide later turns into a chapter "by place" in the family history ([Writing the family history](../3-results/02-family-history.md), section 4).

## 6. Study the whole family, not one ancestor

- **One family at a time.** Crista Cowan (Ancestry's corporate genealogist, "The Barefoot Genealogist") says that jumping around the tree confuses and muddles most of all [checked: organizeyourfamilyhistory.com]. Bring one family to a report, then move on to the next.
- **What to write in the notes on a person** (same place): transcriptions of documents, **unsuccessful searches**, a to-do list and evidence-based reasoning. A source for every piece of information, even if it is "grandmother said".
- **Brothers, sisters, in-laws, witnesses.** A document about your ancestor is often found in the file of his brother or in the record of his sister's wedding. Studying a person apart from the family and neighbours is a typical beginner mistake (FamilySearch Wiki, "Rookie Mistakes") [checked]. Searching the circle is the FAN principle in [Standard of proof](../3-results/01-standard-of-proof.md), section 9.
- Keep the timeline **per family**: the events of all the children in one table at once show where the family lived in which years.
