For English speakers: Russian, Ukrainian and other sources when you don't read them

What this section covers: how to use the Russian and Ukrainian archives and databases in this guide if you don't read Cyrillic, Hebrew or Yiddish — with an AI assistant, browser translation and a few tables. Written for descendants of emigrants in the USA, UK, Canada, Australia, Israel and South America. This page exists only in the English version.

Markers: [checked] — the page was opened and read (October 2026); [from search snippet] — seen in search results only; [unverified] — not checked. More in the Glossary.

If you have never worked with AI before, start with Three levels, level 1: one check, one relative, one evening.

1. What you can do without reading Russian

Honestly: a lot.

Task Can you do it with AI + browser translation? What still needs care
Find which archive and file holds your town's records yes archive names and file titles are mistranslated (section 6)
Search a database (war dead, Holocaust victims, repressed) yes, if you type the name in Cyrillic (section 4) one spelling is never enough (section 3)
Read a printed directory, newspaper or typed list yes, AI reads print well check names letter by letter against the image
Read a handwritten 19th-century metrical book or revision list partly: AI reads the printed form and headings well, handwritten names and places worse always a second check — by you on a zoomed fragment, or by a human reader
Read the Hebrew side of a Jewish metrical record, a gravestone, a Yiddish letter partly ask a human reader for anything you will build on

The rule for everything below: a translation or an AI reading is a lead, not a fact. Before a name goes into your tree, compare it with the scan (see Checking results).

2. The Cyrillic alphabet in one table

You don't need to learn it, but recognising the letters lets you check what the AI read.

Traps for English readers: Cyrillic В is "v", Н is "n", Р is "r", С is "s", У is "u", Х is "kh". So "РОСС" is Ross, not Pocc.

Letters before 1918

Records before the 1918 spelling reform use a few letters that are gone now (FamilySearch Wiki, "Russia Handwriting") [checked via the reference page]:

Old letter Became Example
ѣ (yat) е мѣщанинъ → мещанин (meshchanin, townsman)
і и Іосифъ → Иосиф
ѳ (fita) ф Ѳеодоръ → Федор
ѵ (izhitsa, rare) и
ъ at the end of a word dropped Розенбергъ → Розенберг

Search both spellings: a Google Books search for «Розенбергъ Яковъ» with the final ъ found a directory that two weeks of ordinary searches had missed (Names, surnames, spellings, section 4).

Ukrainian differences

Ukrainian Sounds like Russian equivalent Latin (Ukrainian 2010 system)
г h (Russian г is g) h
ґ g г g
и short "y", like Russian ы ы y

ї yee — yi / i

This is why one town has two spellings: Russian Проскуров (o) — Ukrainian Проскурів (i); Russian Дунаевцы — Ukrainian Дунаївці.

Handwriting look-alikes

In 19th-century cursive, lowercase т often looks like a Latin m, и like u, д has a tail like g, п looks like n, б like the digit 6. Alexander Beider lists pairs that scribes and readers confuse: а/о, г/ч, м/т [checked via the reference page]; others are Б/В, Ш/Т, Р/К. To see what a surname looks like in cursive, type it into Steve Morse's printed-to-cursive tool: stevemorse.org/russian/cyrprintcurs.html [checked]. More — Handwriting, transliteration, dates.

3. One name, many spellings

One family name passed through Russian, Polish and German clerks, its owners wrote it in Yiddish or Hebrew, and in emigration it was written in English. Example from this guide's own research: the surname Фейгель and the given name Шлиома.

Script / setting Surname Given name
Russian, pre-1918 Фейгель Шлиома
Russian, Soviet documents Фейгель often replaced: Соломон (Solomon); the patronymic Шлюмович became Соломонович
Ukrainian Фейгель Шльома [unverified]
BGN/PCGN (JewishGen, US/UK maps) Feygel' / Feigel Shlioma
Library of Congress (ALA-LC) Feĭgelʹ Shlioma
ISO 9 Fejgelʹ Šlioma
Ukrainian national system (2010) Feihel (г → h, soft sign not written) —
Polish-style (Kingdom of Poland, Galicia) Fajgel Szlama [from search snippet]
German-style (emigration, German ports) Feygel, Feigl Schloma [unverified]
Yiddish / Hebrew פייגעל (by Yiddish spelling rules; not seen in a record) [unverified] שלמה — Shloyme (Yiddish), Shlomo (Hebrew)
English (manifests, US/UK records) Feigel, Feiggel, Feigal, Faigel Solomon, Sam, Shloime [unverified]

The variants of the surname in this table were all actually found in records — about 20 in total, including Файгель, Фейггель, Фейгаль, Фейгейль, Faigell, Fogiel (the example surname is anonymized; the spread of variants is real). In JewishGen indexes the family is almost never written FEIGEL. And one emigrant, Moische Feigel, became Max Fairman in the USA — the new name is in the naturalization file (Names, surnames, spellings, sections 2 and 9).

How to search

  1. Search in Cyrillic too. Russian directories are OCR'd in Cyrillic. On Genealogy Indexer, "Feigel" in Latin letters gave 1 hit in the whole database, «Фейгель» 15, «Фейггель» 92 (Names, surnames, spellings, section 4).
  2. Drop the first letter for OCR. Old-book OCR often eats or swaps the first letter: search «ейгель», «озенберг».
  3. Exact first, then phonetic. In databases with sound-alike search, use Daitch–Mokotoff or Beider–Morse, built for Eastern European and Jewish names. Phonetics also misleads: "Fessel" returned Фишель and Фисель, not Фесель. Steve Morse explains Beider–Morse at stevemorse.org/phoneticinfo.htm [checked].
  4. Write the name as it is in the document in your notes, with the transliteration next to it — not instead of it. Don't turn Movsha into Moses (JewishGen transcription rules).

5. Searching Russian and Ukrainian sites

Browser translation (Chrome's "Translate this page") makes menus and results readable. Its limits:

  • what you type into the form stays Cyrillic — the site searches its own data in its own language;
  • it mistranslates archival terms: Ukrainian «справа» (file) becomes "affairs", «фонд» "stock", «опис» "description" (Handwriting, transliteration, dates, section 1). Read file titles in the original, or ask the AI;
  • a surname that is also a word may be translated as the word [unverified];
  • if a form or dropdown behaves oddly on the translated page, switch translation off, run the search, then translate the results.

An AI browser plugin (such as Claude in Chrome) can type the Cyrillic query into the form itself, open each result and make a table — see Three levels, level 1. CAPTCHAs are yours to pass: when a "you are not a robot" check appears, solve it yourself and continue more slowly. Never give the plugin your password (Access, CAPTCHAs, pace).

Reading a citation

ДАХмО 226-80-1177, арк. NN means: State Archive of Khmelnytskyi Oblast (DAKhmO), fond 226, opys 80, sprava 1177, folio NN. On Commons this file is the 1875 census of Jews of Letychiv uezd, split into four PDFs (Т.1–Т.4) [checked].

  • A folio is a numbered leaf: the number is stamped on the front, usually top right. "NN зв." / "NN об." is its back. The folio is not the PDF page number — there are two sides per leaf, plus covers.
  • A fond with Р- (Р-6450) is a Soviet-era fond.
  • The archive is named after the modern oblast, not the old province: Proskurov, Podolia guberniya, is now in Khmelnytskyi Oblast.

7. Dates

  • Russia used the Julian calendar until 1918. Difference: 12 days in the 19th century, 13 from March 1900. Write both: "3/15 March 1875" (Handwriting, transliteration, dates, section 5).
  • Hebrew dates: the day begins at sunset, so one Hebrew date covers two civil days; families remembered birthdays and yahrzeits by the Hebrew calendar, so civil dates in emigration papers are often recalculated from memory. Allow ±1 day. Converter: Hebcal [checked].
  • A boy's birth record often gives the date of circumcision; the birth was 7–8 days earlier.

Tips:

  • Spell the place several ways: on JewishGen "Kamenets-Podolsk" gave 0, "Kamenets" gave 8.
  • Use the JewishGen Gazetteer and Communities Database (jewishgen.org/communities, English) [checked]: sound-alike search for a garbled name; a town's page lists alternative names by language and the district and province around 1900 (for Proskurov: Proskurov uezd, Podolia guberniya) [from search snippet].
  • Check the district, not just the name: two places with one name are common. Record the uezd and guberniya for each period — they decide which archive holds the records.

9. Ready-made prompts

How to ask AI

For a plain chat, add: "If you can't open a source, say so; mark fonds, villages and dates you name from memory as [from memory, verify]. Answer in English, and give names, places and archive titles in the original script in parentheses." (why)

  • Spellings. "My ancestor's surname was written [Feiggel] in a US record; he came from [town] near [city], [Ukraine], about [year]. Give me the likely Cyrillic spellings (Russian, pre-1918 and modern, and Ukrainian), Polish, German and Yiddish forms, variants with another root vowel, and the forms without the first letter for OCR search. Put them in a table I can copy." Check yourself: a form you can't find in Beider's dictionaries (index on stevemorse.org) is probably a misreading.
  • Reading a scan. "This is a [metrical book / revision list / census] of [place], [year]. First pass: read the column headings, then row [N] word for word, keeping names in the original Cyrillic and adding a transliteration; mark anything unreadable as (?). Don't guess names. Second pass: translate into English." Check yourself: compare names and numbers with the zoomed scan; AI tends to see what is expected. Don't tell it the names you hope for in the first pass.
  • Using a site. "I'm on [site]. I don't read Russian. Walk me through searching for [name] born about [year]: which fields to fill, what to type in Cyrillic, what the buttons mean." Check yourself: run a control search for a record you know exists; some forms silently ignore filters.
  • Reading a citation. "Explain this archival reference: [ДАХмО 226-80-1177, арк. NN]. Which archive, what kind of file, how do I find the folio in the scan?" Check yourself: the archive's own inventory or the Commons file title.

Bad → good: "Translate this." (no document type, place or year — the AI reads blind) → "This is an 1875 Jewish census of [uezd]: read row [N] word for word, names in Cyrillic plus transliteration, unreadable as (?)."
