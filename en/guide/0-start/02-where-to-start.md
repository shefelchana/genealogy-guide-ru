# Where to start: the first two weeks

What this section covers: what to do at the very beginning, while nothing has been found yet. How to gather the home archive, interview relatives and choose a first question. A one-page short version — [Quick start](01-quick-start.md).

---

## 1. Gather everything you have at home

Before opening any websites, collect what already lies in the family:

- **photographs**, especially those with captions on the back (who, where, when);
- **documents**: birth, marriage and death certificates, passports, work record books, military ID cards, diplomas, evacuation certificates, notifications of death in action (*pokhoronki*);
- **letters and postcards**: they often carry addresses, dates and names;
- **notebooks, albums, certificates of merit, newspaper clippings**.

Photograph or scan every item. At once write next to it who is in the photograph and where the document came from. In a year there will be no one left to ask.

How to handle originals (advice from the VGD forum — "All-Russian Genealogical Tree", Russian-language, docs.vgd.ru/kak-najti-predkov): do not restore them yourself, do not glue them into albums, and on the back of a photograph write **in pencil** who, where and when.

## 2. Record family memory — as hypotheses, not facts

Interview older relatives. Record **verbatim** and with a note of who remembers it that way and when they told it: "so says Aunt N., conversation of 05.03.2026". The VGD forum advises also recording the circumstances of the telling.

Family memory often errs in dates, confuses generations and changes surnames. But it sometimes contains precise leads. In one real research project the phrase "we had relatives in Iași" led to Bessarabia, and documents were found there.

What they remember about the following is especially valuable:
- **towns and shtetls** (where they were born, where they moved from);
- **occupations** (doctor, merchant, teacher, timber dealer — there are separate reference works for them);
- **wars, evacuation, repression**;
- **relatives abroad** (emigrant documents often name the shtetl and the parents).

If people of the older generation are alive, **talking to them is the first item of the plan**: it cannot be put off (so says the Am HaZikaron institute). Interview even very distant relatives too — they often keep what close ones do not have.

Every such statement is a **hypothesis**. It must be checked against a document. How to do that — in the section [Standard of proof](../3-results/02-standard-of-proof.md). How to prepare for a conversation, what questions to ask and how to record — [Talking to relatives](03-talking-to-relatives.md).

## 3. From the known to the unknown, one generation at a time

This is common advice in all guides: the JewishGen FAQ ("work from the known to the unknown, one small step at a time"), FamilySearch ("Research Process"), the VGD forum.

First reliably establish the parents of the person you know. Then their grandparents. Do not jump straight into the 18th century to a "famous ancestor" with the same surname: without intermediate links that is not kinship but coincidence.

## 4. The main unit of search is the place, not the surname

The shtetl, uezd and guberniya determine **which archive** holds the documents and **which documents** exist at all. Knowing only the country is not enough: you need the uezd and guberniya (so writes the FamilySearch Wiki about Ukraine, Belarus and Moldova).

What to do:
1. Find the birthplace of the oldest person about whom there is a document (passport, certificate, questionnaire, evacuation card).
2. Establish what the place was called then and which uezd and guberniya it belonged to. There are gazetteers for this: the JewishGen Gazetteer and Communities Database, for Belarus — Radzima, for Bessarabia — the Bessarabia Geographical Dictionary, the "Lists of Populated Places of the Russian Empire" (*Spiski naselennykh mest Rossiiskoi imperii*). More — in [Directories and books](../../catalog/directories-and-books.md).
3. Check what survives for this place and where. The order is in [Archives by country](../2-reference/07-archives-by-country.md).

A surname is spelled in different ways, is misrecognized, and before the 19th century Jews often had none at all (see [Names, surnames, spellings](../2-reference/03-names-spellings.md)). The place is more reliable.

## 5. One question at a time

Before every visit to the sites, formulate **one specific question** and what will count as the answer.

- Bad: "find out more about my great-grandfather".
- Good: "find the date of the marriage of my great-grandfather and great-grandmother" (a FamilySearch example, "Research Process").
- Good: "who were the parents of Moisei, born about 1868 in Izmail?"

Advice from the Board for Certification of Genealogists (BCG, the article "Focused versus Diffuse Research"): without a narrow question neither analysis, nor correlation, nor proof is possible. Nadya Lipes also advises stating the aim of the search clearly in advance, so as not to be disappointed later.

How to turn a question into a six-step plan and compile a person's timeline — [Plan, timeline, place guide](04-plan-timeline-place.md).

Decide in advance **when to stop**: "no more than three evenings on this question" or "until I have gone through the 1868 birth book in full". So recommends BCG ("Planning Effective Research"): the plan is ended when the question is resolved, the methods are exhausted, or the allotted time and money have run out.

## 6. Where to start if you have only names

The most accessible layer is the Soviet and military one, the closest in time:
- military databases (Pamyat Naroda, OBD Memorial — both Russian-language);
- Yad Vashem (evacuation cards, Pages of Testimony);
- memorial books, the "Open List" (*Otkrytyi spisok*, Russian-language);
- scans of ZAGS (Soviet civil registry) books of the 1920s–1940s (for Ukraine some are posted on Wikimedia Commons);
- cemetery databases.

Details — [War, repression, the siege](../2-reference/12-war-repression-siege.md). From the oldest person with a known place, step back: metrical books, censuses, revisions of that place ([Records of the Russian Empire and the USSR](../2-reference/01-records-empire-ussr.md)).

If the family has an emigrant branch (USA, Argentina, Palestine), start with it: American documents often name the shtetl directly. See [Emigration](../2-reference/13-emigration.md).

## 7. Typical beginner mistakes

According to observations on the VGD forum:
- information is not systematized, sources are not given;
- the hope that all the relatives will get absorbed (per VGD, about a fifth are interested);
- trying to do everything alone — yet there are forums, volunteers, groups (see [Groups and communities](../../reading/groups-and-communities.md));
- no double-checking of facts.

To this it is worth adding:
- entering everything into an online tree, guesses included;
- not recording where you have already searched — and a month later searching the same place a second time;
- paying for a "genealogical search" for what lies in open access (see [Access, captchas, pace](../2-reference/19-access-captchas-pace.md)).

The full list of 16 mistakes with sources — [Beginner mistakes](../3-results/05-beginner-mistakes.md).

## The outcome of the first two weeks

A "person — dates — place — source" table for each person about whom something is known, plus one formulated question for the first round of search. Lay the events of the era over the table — wars, pogroms, evacuation, border changes: some of the legends will immediately stop fitting by date, and it will become clear which documents are missing (advice from N. Lipes).

If you work with an AI assistant, give it this table and ask it to propose a first question and a plan — [Prompt library](../1-ai-search/02-prompt-library.md), section "Start". Templates are in [../../templates/](../../templates/finding.md).
