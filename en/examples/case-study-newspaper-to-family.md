# Case study: from a newspaper advertisement to a family

What this section covers: a short connected example from a real research project (October 2026) — how one find leads to the next, where a check against the scan was needed and where the conclusion stayed a hypothesis. The chain: an advertisement of 1887 → a correspondence of the same year → a marriage record of the 1890s ("also Grinshtein") → the signature under an article of 1884 → a death certificate in Los Angeles.

All the people in the example died long ago. Names, surnames, the town and archival references are changed; the sequence of finds, the checks and the traps are real.

---

## The starting point

A doctor was known, Yakov Berkovich Rosenberg, a physician born in the 1860s in a port town in southern Bessarabia (below — "the town"). From 1856 to 1878 this part of Bessarabia belonged to Romania, so there is no Russian metrical record of his birth and there cannot be. The question of the round: **who was his father Berko, and who was he connected with in the town?**

The method: look for the family where it could have left a trace "on another occasion" — in Jewish newspapers. A search in the Historical Jewish Press of the National Library of Israel: the surname in Yiddish and Hebrew in several spellings **plus** the name of the town in several spellings (in Hebrew it had two). Don't read Russian? See [For English speakers](../guide/0-start/08-for-english-speakers.md).

## Step 1. The advertisement of 1887

**Found:** "Yidishes folksblat" and "Ha-Tsfira", 1887 (the same request in Hebrew; issue and page are in the research log). The committee of the synagogue at the town's Talmud Torah asks to print an advertisement of a "poor *agunah*": her husband Motl Grinshtain took the money, jewelry and dresses and fled, leaving her pregnant. Description: short, fair-haired, big blue eyes; rumor says which road he took. Telegrams are asked to be sent to "my *shvoger* Beril Rosenberg"; in a footnote: "The cost of the telegrams is on me, Beril Rosenberg"; the signature — "On behalf of the unfortunate woman — B. Rosenberg".

**Checked against the scan:** the recognized text of the newspaper found the article, but the quotation and the translation were made **from the image**. The Yiddish version gave a detail that is not in the Hebrew one: the *agunah* is pregnant.

**What this gave:** Beril Rosenberg in 1887 is a settled resident of the town, with money, connected with community institutions. Beril and Berko are everyday forms of one Jewish name (Dov-Ber), so he is a candidate for Yakov's father; but a match of name and town is not yet proof. For the first time a person from the documents came alive.

**What stayed open:** a *shvoger* (brother-in-law) — is he her sister's husband or her husband's brother? The word does not settle it.

## Step 2. A correspondence of the same year

If the newspaper has an advertisement, there might be a note too. A search for the runaway's surname (in all spellings in Yiddish) over all years gave exactly three articles — all about this case.

**Found:** "Yidishes folksblat", 1887, the rubric "Correspondence", "[the town] (Bessarabia), July 1887", signed with a pseudonym. The correspondent mocks the fashion of marrying **without a matchmaker**: "the cavalier of today's fashion" presented himself to the bride's family as a decent man, got the money, took his young wife to where **his parents** lived, drove her out pregnant, and after the court (he was ordered to pay 500 rubles a year) persuaded her to sell her things and ran away with the furniture, clothes and jewelry. The name is given: Motl Grinshtain, 26 years old.

**Checked against the scan:** the correspondence was read in full from the image and translated completely; one faded word is marked as uncertain.

**What this changed:** Motl is an outsider, his parents live not in the town; the bride is "of a respectable family" in the town. This tilts toward the reading "Beril is her sister's husband", not "her husband's brother". **The second document changed the interpretation of the first** — which is why the first guess should not have been entered into the tree at once.

## Step 3. A marriage record of the 1890s: "also Grinshtein"

The next step is the indexes of Bessarabian metrical books on JewishGen. The newspaper spelling "Grinshtain" together with the town — zero. But a similar entry turned up: a marriage in Chișinău in the 1890s, the groom from the town — "ROZENBERG / GRINSHTEIN Khuna".

**Checked against the scan — and a correction of the index.** From the index it was first read as "Khuna, son of Abram". The scan of the marriage book (ANRM — National Archives of the Republic of Moldova — f. NNN, op. NN, d. NNN, entry no. NNN, FamilySearch, frame NNN) showed: "Unmarried resident of [the town] **Khuna Mikhelev Rosenberg /also Grinshtein/**" (Холостъ …скій житель Хуна Михелевъ Розенбергъ /онъ же Гринштейнъ/), the bride — daughter of the Chișinău townsman (*meshchanin*) **Abram** Kaplan. Abram is the bride's father, not the groom's. The index mixed up the fields.

**Confirmation from an independent source:** the directory "All Russia" of the early 1900s — "Grinshtein (Rosenberg) Khuna Mikhel." (Гринштейн (Розенберг) Хуна Михел.).

**What this gave:** it is proved that Grinshtein is a second name of one of the town's Rosenberg families (the line of Mikhel). This is a strong sign: such a pairing is rare.

**What stayed a hypothesis:** whether "Grinshtain" from the newspaper and "Grinshtein" from the metrical record are one family. There is no document that would link them. So only the fact of the marriage goes into the tree; the link with the runaway of 1887 goes into the hypotheses file.

⚠️ **The trap of expectation.** In another record of the same circle a scribe's "Mikhel Gr…shtein" (Михель Гр…штейнъ) was very tempting to read as "Grinshtein". On enlargement it turned out to be "Gorshtein" (Горштейнъ). When you are looking for a specific name, the eye "completes" it — enlarge and compare the letters with the neighboring records by the same scribe.

## Step 4. The signature under an article of 1884

Further searching in the same newspaper (surname + town, neighboring years) found an article ("Yidishes folksblat", 1884) with the signature **"Eliezer Moshkov Rosenberg. …, March 1884"** (the place is the same town in southern Bessarabia). In the annual index of correspondents for 1883 the same author ("Rosenberg, E. Moshkov, from [the town]") is listed with one more note.

**Checked against the scan:** the signature was read from the image. "Moshkov" is a patronymic in Yiddish: son of Moshe.

**What this gave:** in the town in the 1880s there lived a literate author Eliezer (Leizer), son of Moshe Rosenberg. A new person — and a new thread.

## Step 5. A death certificate in Los Angeles

Among natives of the town in American documents one was already known, Leizer Rosenberg, who died in Los Angeles in the 1940s. According to American documents, above all the death certificate: born in the town around 1858; father — Morris (the American form of the name Moshe); occupation — teacher of languages (25 years).

**Checked against the scan:** the father's name and place of birth in the death certificate must be read from the image of the form, not from the index: indexes of American documents also make mistakes (see [Emigration](../guide/2-reference/13-emigration.md)).

**Comparison:**

| Feature | Newspaper 1884 | Certificate (1940s) |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Eliezer | Leizer (Leizer is an everyday form of Eliezer) |
| Father | Moshkov — son of Moshe | Morris = Moshe |
| Town | the town | born in the town |
| Period | 1883–1884, an adult author | born about 1858 — in 1884 he is about 26 |
| Occupation | writes articles | teacher of languages |

**Conclusion:** a strong link — five features match, and the sources were created independently (a 19th-century Jewish newspaper and a 20th-century American form). But there is no direct document saying "this is one person"; the data of the death certificate were supplied by an informant who was a relative (secondary information). So the honest status is **a hypothesis with a strong basis**, not "proved"; such a conclusion is worth setting out in a written argument (see [Standard of proof](../guide/3-results/01-standard-of-proof.md)). How Leizer is related to Berko has not yet been established.

## What this example teaches

1. **Place matters more than surname.** A search for "Rosenberg" without the town would give thousands of matches; the pairing "surname + town" — a few dozen.
2. **One find suggests the next:** the runaway's name → a search on it → the metrical index → a double surname; neighboring issues of the newspaper → a new author → an American document.
3. **An index is a hint.** The index mixed up the bride's father with the groom's father; only the scan caught the error.
4. **A second document can overturn the interpretation of the first** (the correspondence changed the meaning of the word *shvoger*).
5. **Not everything is proved.** Honest statuses — "proved", "strong hypothesis", "open" — matter more than a beautiful story.
6. **Every step is recorded at once:** the archival reference, frame, quotation, translation, confidence of the reading ([finding.md](../templates/finding.md)), empty results go into the research log (for example, the newspaper spelling of the runaway's surname in JewishGen — 0).
